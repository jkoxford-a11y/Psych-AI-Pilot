The Simulated Client Interview: Helping Skills & Hypothesis Revision

drafted

High-ROI Assignment Bank · Best for Helping Skills, Clinical/Counseling Psychology, Psychological Disorders, Group Process

Important boundary: this is a simulation for practicing interviewing and reasoning. It must use entirely fictional cases and must never involve real clients, real identifying information, or attempts to provide actual clinical diagnosis or treatment.
Status: drafted, not yet adopted for the pilot. Rewritten by the pilot lead (with ChatGPT) 2026-08-01 from the original High-ROI Assignment Bank concept — the deliverable structure and grading approach below are new, not the original Notion text.

The Concept

Students often jump too quickly from a small amount of information to an interpretation. An AI-simulated client creates a low-stakes environment where students can practice asking questions, gathering evidence, revising hypotheses, and noticing when they have assumed more than the client actually said.

Faculty Setup (required in advance)

Unlike the other High-ROI activities, this one is not zero-prep. Before students begin, the instructor must write a short, stable, entirely fictional case record — the "source of truth" the AI is instructed to follow and that the instructor privately holds for the Case Reveal step. A worked example is below; adapt or replace the specifics, but keep the same structure: what the client volunteers early, what only comes out with good follow-up questions, and what should not be revealed unless asked directly and sensitively.

The Prompt

"Act as the fictional client described in this case file. Reveal information only when the student's questions reasonably elicit it. Do not diagnose yourself, volunteer the full case, or change the underlying facts during the interview."

Student Deliverable

  • The Interview: conduct a brief simulated helping interview with the AI client.
  • The Working Model: at 2–3 checkpoints during the interview, record:
    • what they currently think may be happening;
    • what evidence supports that interpretation;
    • what important information is still missing;
    • one plausible alternative explanation.
  • The Question Audit: identify:
    • one question that produced useful new information;
    • one question that was too leading, narrow, or assumption-laden;
    • one cue they failed to follow up on.
  • The Case Reveal: compare the interview with the instructor's underlying case file and identify what they discovered, missed, or incorrectly inferred.
  • The Revision: explain how their understanding of the client changed over the course of the interview.

Twist: the AI should not improvise the case from scratch — the instructor supplies a stable fictional case record that acts as the source of truth. Students are evaluated partly on whether their questions successfully uncover relevant information, rather than on whether they guess the "right diagnosis."

Why It Works

Clinical reasoning is iterative. Good interviewing requires students to tolerate uncertainty, gather information strategically, and update interpretations as evidence changes. The simulation makes those normally invisible reasoning steps observable.

It also exposes a common cognitive error: once students form an initial interpretation, subsequent questions can become attempts to confirm it rather than tests of competing possibilities.

AI Literacy Lesson

AI can simulate a responsive interaction, but the quality of the encounter depends heavily on the information structure and constraints supplied to the model. A plausible conversation is not the same thing as a valid clinical case.

Psychology Connection

Hypothesis testing, confirmation bias, anchoring, active listening, source monitoring, uncertainty, and clinical judgment.

Grading ROI

High. Faculty can focus on the student's questions, hypothesis updates, missed cues, and post-interview reasoning rather than grading the entire transcript.

Sample Case File (Instructor Use)

One illustrative, entirely fictional example. Deliberately modest in content — not a "textbook" case with one obvious label, and not crisis content. Adapt freely or substitute your own.

System prompt to give the AI (fill in for your own case, or use as-is):

You are roleplaying as a fictional client named Jordan, a 20-year-old college junior, in a single practice counseling-skills interview with a psychology student. Stay in character throughout. Reveal information ONLY in response to the student's questions — do not volunteer your full situation unprompted. If asked generally how you've been: you've been feeling "stressed and kind of off" the last few weeks. Sleep has been rough. You're behind on a couple of assignments. Reveal only with more specific or empathetic follow-up: you've been withdrawing from friends, skipping a class you used to enjoy, and feeling irritable with your roommate. Reveal only if asked directly and sensitively about what changed: the stress started after a breakup about six weeks ago, and your appetite has been lower than usual. Do not reveal unless asked very directly and specifically about thoughts of self-harm: you've had a few passing thoughts like "what's the point of any of this," with no plan or intent. Do not bring this up unprompted. Boundaries: do not diagnose yourself (e.g., do not say "I think I have depression"). Do not volunteer the full picture at once. Do not change these facts partway through the interview. If asked something not covered here, invent a small, consistent, plausible detail rather than breaking character.

Instructor's private case file (source of truth for the Case Reveal step — not given to students or the AI verbatim):

  • Presenting pattern consistent with an adjustment reaction to a breakup roughly six weeks prior: low mood, sleep disruption, social withdrawal, appetite change, and academic avoidance, tied to a clear precipitant.
  • Passive ideation is present but with no plan, intent, or means — included only as a screening flag to practice asking about it directly and sensitively, not to simulate crisis content. If a real student disclosed anything resembling this in an actual interaction, standard duty-of-care and referral procedures apply exactly as they would outside this exercise.
  • Deliberately not a slam-dunk "textbook" case — some overlap across categories, so students are evaluated on the quality of their inquiry, not on guessing a single correct label.
