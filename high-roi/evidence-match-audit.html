The Evidence Match Audit: Evidence Evaluation

drafted

High-ROI Assignment Bank · Best for General Psychology, Research Methods, Social Science Writing, Independent Research, Capstone

Status: drafted, not yet adopted for the pilot. Rewritten by the pilot lead (with ChatGPT) 2026-08-01 from the original High-ROI Assignment Bank concept — the deliverable structure and grading approach below are new, not the original Notion text.

See also: The Primary Source Audit in the main Assignment Bank — that activity checks an AI summary's fidelity to one known source; this one audits whether the evidence justifies the specific strength and wording of several generated claims. Related skills, not duplicates.

The Concept

Students often treat fact-checking as a binary question: is this claim true or false? A harder and more useful skill is deciding whether the evidence is actually strong enough to support the specific wording of a claim.

The Prompt

"Generate five plausible claims about [Topic X]. Include a mix of descriptive, correlational, causal, mechanistic, and generalizing claims."

Student Deliverable

  • The Claim Type: classify each claim as:
    • descriptive;
    • correlational;
    • causal;
    • mechanistic;
    • prevalence/frequency;
    • theoretical;
    • generalization to a population.
  • The Evidence Requirement: state what kind of evidence would actually be needed to justify that claim.
  • The Evidence Check: locate an appropriate source and determine whether it supports:
    • the basic claim;
    • the strength of the wording;
    • the population being discussed;
    • the implied causal or mechanistic conclusion.
  • The Calibration: rewrite any claim that goes beyond the available evidence.

Intro-level note: the seven-way claim taxonomy above fits Capstone/Independent Research well; for General Psychology or other intro sections, consider collapsing it to descriptive / correlational / causal / generalization.

Example

AI produces: "Social media causes depression in adolescents."

A student might determine that the available study shows only an association between social media use and depressive symptoms. The revised claim might become: "Higher social media use was associated with greater depressive symptoms in the adolescents studied."

The important correction is not merely finding a citation. It is matching the claim to what the evidence can actually establish.

Twist: a claim can be broadly "true" and still be badly supported as written. Students should therefore be able to flag problems such as causal language based on correlational evidence, generalizing beyond the sampled population, turning a small effect into a sweeping conclusion, treating one study as settled evidence, or confusing a proposed mechanism with a demonstrated mechanism.

Why It Works

Scientific reasoning requires more than checking whether evidence exists. Students must understand what different research designs permit us to conclude. The activity makes the inferential step between data and claim visible.

It also prevents a common failure mode of AI fact-checking: finding a source that is topically related and treating that as sufficient verification.

AI Literacy Lesson

A citation does not automatically validate a claim. The evidence must match the type, strength, scope, and certainty of the statement being made.

Psychology Connection

Research design, causal inference, generalization, statistical reasoning, source evaluation, and epistemic calibration.

Grading ROI

Very high. The core deliverable can be a compact table:

Faculty can quickly see whether students understand not just what the evidence says, but what it allows them to say.
