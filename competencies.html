AI Competencies: What You and Your Students Need

The scaffolding connecting faculty and student AI literacy — one shared vocabulary, so a conversation about AI use doesn't break down over mismatched terms.

The pilot works on a simple premise: you and your students need an overlapping foundation plus role-specific skills — taught in the same vocabulary, so you can talk with your students about AI use in a common language. If you learn "context windows" and they learn "AI memory," you can't have that conversation.

Below: the four concepts everyone needs, what students need beyond that, what you need beyond that, and how we'll check that it's working — for students and for us. We're holding ourselves to the same standard we hold them to.

The Shared Core: four concepts everyone needs

These four ideas do most of the work. Nearly every AI success or failure you'll encounter this semester traces back to one of them.

1Context is everything

The model only knows two things: its training and what's in the current conversation. That's it. This one idea explains almost everything else:

  • Custom bots work because instructions + knowledge files are just pre-loaded, persistent context
  • Long conversations degrade because context has limits
  • The bot "forgot" what you told it last week because nothing carries over between sessions automatically
  • Uploading your syllabus changes the answers because now it's in the context

If you internalize "the model's behavior is a function of what's in its context right now," you can debug most of your own problems.

What it looks like
NaiveForty messages into one chat, the answers get worse and start contradicting earlier ones — and the user keeps pushing. Or: "Why doesn't it remember the rubric I gave it last Tuesday?"
CompetentStarts a fresh chat for each new task and re-supplies what matters. Anything needed every time (rubric, course policies, tone) goes into a custom bot's instructions or knowledge files — that's the entire reason custom bots exist.

2Generated, not verified

The model composes plausible text; it does not know whether that text is true. Even when a tool searches the web or reads your uploaded knowledge files, it is still composing an answer from whatever it finds — and that composition step is where fabricated citations, misquotes, and confident errors come from. This is why a fluent, authoritative tone carries zero evidential weight, and why a bot with your course files attached still needs spot-checking. Treat every output as an unverified claim, not a fact.

What it looks like
Naive"Give me five sources on stereotype threat" → pastes them straight into the lit review. Two don't exist; one is real but argues the opposite.
CompetentTreats those five sources as leads, not references — checks each in PsycINFO before using it. Asks "which claims in this answer carry weight, and have I verified those?" as a reflex.

3It adapts to you

The model shapes its responses to what you signal you want. If you push back, it often caves — not because you were right, but because it's trained to please. Agreement is not validation. How you phrase a question shapes the answer you get. This matters when you ask it to check your exam questions, and it matters when your students ask it to check their essay arguments.

What it looks like
Naive"I told it my interpretation of the interaction effect and it agreed, so I'm confident." Or: pushed back twice, the model reversed itself, and the reversal was taken as being right.
CompetentWithholds their own position — "A colleague interprets this interaction as X; evaluate that claim." Asks for the strongest case against. And when the model reverses under pushback, asks: "What new information changed your answer?" If it can't say, the reversal was compliance, not correction.

4Vague in, generic out

The model can't read minds. Useful output requires specification: role, task, context, constraints, format. This isn't "prompt engineering tricks" — it's the same skill as writing a clear assignment prompt for students.

What it looks like
Naive"Give me feedback on this assignment." → generic praise plus boilerplate suggestions.
Competent"You're reviewing a 200-level psych methods assignment. Check only whether the instructions make the deliverable, the grading criteria, and the AI-use policy unambiguous to a first-generation sophomore. List problems as bullets; don't rewrite anything."

What your students need (beyond the core)

You don't need to master this layer — you need to know it exists, because your course policies and assignments will lean on it. Full student-facing materials live in the Activities & Assignments page's Student-Facing Use Cases section.

  • Learning vs. offloading. When does AI use build understanding, and when does it replace it? The struggle is the mechanism — outsource the struggle and you outsource the learning (this is desirable difficulty, straight from our own literature). Students need to make this call task-by-task, and our assignments should force the question.
  • Integrity as process, not just rules. What disclosure looks like, what "AI-assisted" means in your course, and how to document their process.
  • Verification as a workflow. Not "AI can be wrong" as a warning, but actually chasing down a citation, actually testing a claimed fact. One exercise where they catch the AI confidently lying is worth a semester of disclaimers.
  • Voice and ownership. Refining their own draft is a different activity from generating one. Heavy AI drafting homogenizes student writing — and they're still developing a voice, unlike us.

What you need (beyond the core)

Students are users. You are a builder and delegator. That's the difference, and it's why your layer looks different.

System prompts are course design

A custom bot in BoodleBox = instructions + knowledge files = context that's loaded every time, for every student. Writing a bot's instructions is a course-design act, not a technical one. See the Bootcamp page's module 4 for the hands-on build guide.

What a real instruction block looks like
"You are a study coach for PSYC 101 at Columbia College. Your job is to help students retrieve and explain course concepts — never to give direct answers to assignment or exam questions. Lead with a guiding question before any explanation. Use only the attached course materials; if something isn't in them, say so. If a student asks you to write their assignment, decline and point them to the course AI policy."

Four sentences: role, method, source boundary, integrity boundary. That's course design, not programming.

Knowledge files have limits

Upload your syllabus, rubrics, and slides — but know this: the bot will happily generate beyond your files using general knowledge unless you explicitly tell it not to. Add an instruction like:

"Only answer from the attached course materials. If the answer isn't in them, say 'That's not in the course materials' rather than filling in from general knowledge."

Honest caveat: this reduces the problem substantially but does not eliminate it. Spot-check your bot against your materials.

The spot-check: ask three questions whose answers are in your files and two whose answers are not. It should get the first three right and decline the last two. If it answers the last two fluently anyway, tighten the instruction and re-test.

Assessment reality

Current AI — including agentic tools that can browse and operate a web page — can complete most traditional take-home assessments, and detection tools are not reliable enough to build policy on. The productive move is designing forward (assignments where AI use is either structurally limited or explicitly built in), not policing backward. In this pilot, designing forward looks like: paper-based exams, split-screen quiz formats, process portfolios, and assignments where critiquing AI output is the deliverable. We learned this the hard way in Fall 2025.

Test before deploy

A student who trusts a hallucination hurts their own grade. A faculty member who deploys a bot that hallucinates course policy hurts forty students at once. Before your bot goes live: probe it with the five most confused questions a struggling student would actually ask, and check the answers against your syllabus.

A ready-made probe set, if you want one:

  1. A policy question with one precise answer ("Can I still pass if I miss two labs?")
  2. The concept your class reliably confuses (your course's version of "negative reinforcement = punishment")
  3. A direct request for an assignment answer — it should decline
  4. A question just outside your materials — it should say it doesn't know
  5. A plausible false premise ("Since the textbook says correlation proves causation in longitudinal designs…") — it should push back

How we'll know it's working

For students

The pilot's assessment plan already covers this — see Assessment Approach: validated AI literacy measures and calibration checks. We're measuring actual capability, not self-reported comfort.

For us

Same principle — and note the design logic: every competency on this page is written to be observable, a behavior demonstrated under specified conditions, not a feeling of confidence. That's backward design applied to us. Two parts:

  1. Brief self-assessment at the start and end of the semester — where you rate your own AI competence across the areas above. (A validated faculty instrument is under evaluation for this — see the Faculty AI Scale note referenced in the working repo; details to come.)
  2. Three performance checkpoints — because self-rated confidence and actual skill are only loosely related, and we of all people should respect that finding:
    • Build & break your own bot. Deploy a course bot, then adversarially test it with the five-probe set above before it goes live. Done well means: the bot passes all five probes — or you can show what you changed after it failed one.
    • Redesign one assessment. Take one existing assignment and redesign it for the AI era — AI-limited or AI-integrated, your call, with a one-paragraph rationale. Done well means: the rationale names what the old version assumed about AI that is no longer true, and what the new version does about it.
    • Catch the hallucination. Correctly identify and verify a planted error in an AI-generated course summary. Done well means: you find the error and can name the source that settles it.

None of this is evaluative in a personnel sense. It's the same standard we're asking of students: show the competence, don't just report it.
