Responsible Use

A few habits matter more than a long rulebook.

Learning vs. offloading

Some struggle is the mechanism that builds the skill. If attempting the problem, retrieving the idea, or constructing the explanation is what you are supposed to learn, do that part before handing it to AI.

Habit: write your own attempt first, even a bad one, before asking AI — then use it to check or extend what you did, not replace it.

Verification

AI output is generated, not automatically verified. Check claims in proportion to their consequence — a low-stakes wording suggestion needs less checking than a citation, health claim, statistical interpretation, or factual claim you will submit in academic work. When something matters, ask the bot to search and cite its sources, then open a link and confirm it actually says what was claimed.

Habit: fluency and confidence are not evidence — ask for a source you can check.

Privacy

BoodleBox states it is FERPA-compliant for institutional education use. Still, share personal academic information only when it's genuinely useful for the task — a tutor bot may need to know your course or grade level, not your full record. Never put medical, clinical, financial, password/account, or identifiable client, patient, or research-participant information into a course bot.

Habit: if it's not your own academic record, would be harmful if exposed, or isn't yours to share, do not upload it.

Academic integrity

AI rules can differ by course and assignment. Follow the instructor’s policy and any disclosure or process requirements. You remain responsible for work submitted under your name.

Habit: when the rules are unclear, ask before submitting.

Environmental concerns

AI use has real computing and energy costs. The exact impact varies by model, provider, hardware, and data center, so simple universal numbers are usually misleading.

Habit: use the simplest model that reliably does the job, plan before repeatedly regenerating, and do not confuse easy access with zero resource cost.

Choose the model for the task

Fast, smaller models can be efficient for simple tasks. For difficult reasoning or information where mistakes matter, use a stronger model. Stronger models can still be wrong.

Habit: if you're using whatever model happened to be open, that's the sign to stop and pick on purpose — especially before something that counts.
