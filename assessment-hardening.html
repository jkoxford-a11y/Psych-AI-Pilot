Assessment Hardening for the AI Era

drafted

A practical way to decide whether an assessment still tells you what a student can do — and what to do about it when it doesn't. Not a guide to catching cheating.

AI can now produce credible versions of many traditional take-home assignments. That makes a polished paper, discussion post, or lab report weaker evidence of student capability than it used to be. Trying to solve that with AI detection creates a cat-and-mouse problem and doesn't restore assessment validity. A better question is what evidence would actually convince you a student has the capability you're grading.

An assessment is hardened when a student's grade does not depend primarily on an artifact that AI could produce without the student possessing the intended competence.

Read everything below as answering that question, not "how do I stop students from using AI."

Two kinds of evidence, used deliberately

Use open work where AI can be used productively, and protected work where you can establish what a student can actually do. Not every assignment needs protection; every important course outcome needs enough protected evidence to support the grade. That's a floor, not a ban — most courses should keep AI-assisted practice work alongside a smaller amount of fully protected evidence, not replace one with the other.

Several institutions have built formal vocabulary around this same split — the University of Auckland's "Two-Lane Approach" (controlled/uncontrolled assessment, mandatory programme-wide by 2027) is the clearest example. This pilot doesn't add a second vocabulary on top of Literacy/Fluency/Proficiency; the distinction matters more than the label.

No format has an intrinsic tier

Literacy, Fluency, and Proficiency (see Competencies) describe developmental levels of student capability, not properties of an assessment format. A structured oral or a sealed-source synthesis can probe anything from basic recall through expert judgment, depending on the questions asked and the rubric applied. Set the tier through what you ask, not through which format you pick.

A broader principle sits under "Observed practical": where the disciplinary learning outcome is something students do, assess the doing directly rather than asking for prose describing the doing. That may be the highest-leverage single move on this page for any course that already has a performance to observe.

Looks protected but isn't

  • AI detection tools. Cat-and-mouse, not evidence.
  • Personalization alone. Access history or a personalized prompt doesn't verify anything by itself without a check against it.
  • Portfolio or process logs without verification. Drafts, prompts, and reflections can be generated or reconstructed after the fact — useful as evidence, not as authentication.
  • Recorded, scripted responses without adaptive questioning. If the follow-up doesn't depend on what the student just said, it isn't testing anything the student didn't already prepare.

Not recommended for this pilot

AI-administered or AI-graded protected assessment. NYU Stern's oral-exam system is a real, documented case — personalized case assignment, randomized scenarios, adaptive follow-up questioning, and audio/video capture, graded by a three-model panel, with reported grader agreement of Krippendorff's α = 0.86. That's a genuine security mechanism, not theater, and doesn't belong in the category above. The objection here is different: it hands the consequential evaluative judgment to AI, which conflicts with this pilot's own standard that AI checking AI is not verification. The source material also describes its own deployment as a field report, with transferability to other institutions still an open, empirical question — a further reason not to adopt the mechanics wholesale even where the objection above didn't apply.

Worked examples in this pilot's courses

  • PSYC 210 (Clinical Helping Skills), PSYC 230 (Educational Psychology), PSYC 399 / 499 (Psychology Internship / Advanced Internship) — natural fits for observed practical / simulation. Each already has a real performance to observe rather than only a write-up describing one.
  • PSYC 327 (Qualitative Research Methods) — natural fit for locally generated evidence + provenance check. The course already involves participant observation, interviewing, and field notes; the addition is asking the student to account for one randomly selected observation.
  • PSYC 495W (Integrative Psychology / Capstone) — already structured as portfolio + sampled checkpoint defense (AI Methods Appendix, oral defense, portfolio completion); this menu just names the pattern so it can be reused earlier in the curriculum, not only at the capstone.

How to use this

  1. Find the row closest to what you already assign — this is a lookup, not a mandate to redesign everything.
  2. Confirm the "what it actually demonstrates" column matches what you intend to grade. If the protection mechanism would mean asking AI to check the work, or the demonstrated evidence is trivial, the format isn't doing its job.
  3. Not every graded component needs to move to fully protected — enough protected evidence to reveal what a student can do without outsourcing judgment, not a ban on AI-assisted practice work alongside it.
  4. Record what you actually did on your course's Progress Report page, in the "Assessment Protection Fidelity" section.
