Columbia College Psychology · Fall 2026 pilot

Practicing with AI can leave you worse without it.

That's not a hunch — it's an experimental finding. This pilot exists to make sure our psychology students build the opposite habit: judgment that survives when the AI is gone.

Our students are graduating into a job market that has changed faster than our curriculum. AI skills now top the list of what employers say they cannot find — not as a preference, but as a hiring requirement. Workers without these skills face the highest layoff risk of any group. This is not a future concern; our current students are competing for jobs where this gap already matters.

There's a second reality worth naming honestly: generic AI training doesn't work well. Most workers who receive it can't connect what they learned to their actual job, because the training was disconnected from the work itself. Discipline-embedded AI education — built into courses students are already taking — is what actually produces transferable competence. That's what we're building.

The mission

The Columbia College Psychology program is integrating AI across the curriculum in a deliberate, discipline-specific way. Our goal is not merely to teach students to use AI tools. Our goal is to develop psychologically informed AI users — graduates who can evaluate AI output scientifically, document their reasoning transparently, preserve human judgment in high-stakes decisions, and bring psychological expertise to a workforce that increasingly depends on AI systems it does not fully understand.

Why psychology

Psychology is uniquely positioned for this work. The discipline already studies cognition, bias, perception, social influence, measurement, ethics, and human judgment — the same capacities that determine whether AI is used responsibly or not. AI integration is not an addition to our curriculum. It is an application of it. Every psychology course already teaches students to ask: Is this evidence valid? What assumptions are being made? Where could bias enter? Who is affected by this conclusion? Those are exactly the questions that belong pointed at AI output.

The principle that makes this work

If our assessments of knowledge are genuinely safe from AI, we can let students use AI freely while learning. In that environment the incentive structure changes: AI use that merely produces polished text won't help on an AI-resilient assessment — but AI use that improves recall, explanation, transfer, and disciplinary judgment will.

A pre-registered randomized controlled trial with nearly 1,000 students found that students using AI during practice improved dramatically — but when AI was removed for an unassisted exam, they performed worse than students who had never used AI at all. The tool produced an illusion of competence; the protected assessment revealed the difference. Bastani et al., 2025, PNAS

Our design is built to prevent exactly that outcome — and it's the reason for the headline above.

What this is — and what it is not

AI as only a cheating problemAI as a learning, judgment, and assessment-design problem
Prompt engineering as a standalone skillPsychology-informed evaluation of AI output
AI replacing student thinkingAI supporting preparation after students attempt the task
Detection and surveillance as the main responseAssessment design, transparency, and human defense of reasoning
One-off AI assignmentsMulti-year developmental competence
Generic AI literacyDiscipline-specific AI judgment

This is a first-year pilot. We do not yet have outcome data — we're designing rigorously so the data we collect this Fall will be interpretable, not asserting in advance that the approach works.
