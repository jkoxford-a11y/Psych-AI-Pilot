Scaffolding AI Literacy: Ethics, Privacy and Judgment

Unit 5 of 6 · Tier: Fluency

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for teaching students to use AI with ethical awareness, including privacy, data practices, disclosure, accountability, and environmental responsibility. It is part of the Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for COLL133 and adaptable across disciplines.

Each activity follows the same logic: students encounter a real tension or trade-off first, then develop a personal framework for navigating it. Ethical reasoning isn't a rule to memorize — it's a habit built through practice.

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases or practices students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

Activities can be run in sequence as a full unit or selected individually based on course needs. All work in-person or async with minor adjustments noted.

Privacy and Data Practices

AI tools process everything you give them. Students need to understand what that means before they share sensitive information with any AI system.

Activity 1: What Did You Just Hand Over?

What students do
Students review the privacy policy and terms of service for one AI tool they already use (ChatGPT, Claude, Google Gemini — their choice). They answer four questions in writing: (1) Does the company use your conversations to train future models? Under what conditions? (2) How long is your data retained? (3) What happens to your data if you delete your account? (4) Is there any category of information the policy specifically warns you not to share? Students share findings across the class and compare across platforms.

Why it works
Most students have never read an AI privacy policy. The act of reading one — even briefly — produces genuine surprise, and surprise is what makes the lesson stick. Students also discover that policies differ meaningfully between platforms, which builds the habit of checking rather than assuming.

Debrief question
Based on what you found, what kinds of information would you now handle differently when using this tool?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Before pasting any document into AI, ask: does this contain information about a real person who hasn't consented to this?
  • Keep a personal "do not paste" list: medical records, student data, other people's private communications.
  • Use AI platforms with stronger privacy defaults for sensitive professional work.
Activity 2: The Anonymization Test

What students do
Students are given a realistic scenario: a classmate asks for help drafting an email about a conflict with a roommate, and pastes detailed personal information into the AI prompt — names, the roommate's mental health history, family situation. Students rewrite the prompt to accomplish the same goal while anonymizing or removing identifying details. Then they discuss: what information was actually necessary for the task?

Why it works
Students often share far more personal detail with AI than the task requires — about themselves and others. This activity makes the distinction between necessary and unnecessary disclosure concrete. The roommate scenario is deliberately third-party: students are reasoning about someone else's information, which tends to produce clearer ethical thinking than reasoning about their own.

Debrief question
Was the final product worse because you removed the identifying details? What does that tell you about how much personal information most tasks actually require?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Here is a situation involving a person — I've removed identifying details. Help me think through how to approach it."
  • Before submitting a prompt: ask whether any detail in it identifies a real person who didn't choose to be part of this conversation.

Disclosure and Academic Integrity

The question isn't just whether to use AI — it's how to be transparent about it in ways that are honest and appropriate to the context.

Activity 3: The Disclosure Spectrum

What students do
Students are given five scenarios describing different levels of AI use on an academic task: (1) Used AI to check grammar on a final draft. (2) Used AI to brainstorm an outline, then wrote the paper themselves. (3) Used AI to generate a first draft, then substantially rewrote it. (4) Used AI to generate a draft and made light edits. (5) Submitted AI output with no modification. Working in small groups, students place each scenario on a spectrum from "no disclosure needed" to "full disclosure required" — and defend their placement. Then the class compares, and the instructor reveals the actual course policy and any institutional policy.

Why it works
Disclosure norms are genuinely contested and context-dependent, and students benefit from reasoning through the gradient before being handed a rule. The activity also surfaces the implicit assumption that AI use is binary (used it / didn't use it), when in practice it exists on a continuum. Students who reason through the gradient are better prepared to apply any policy to novel situations.

Debrief question
Where on the spectrum did your group disagree most? What principle were you actually arguing about?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • When in doubt about disclosure, err toward transparency — a brief note about AI use costs nothing and protects you.
  • Draft a personal AI use statement you could attach to any assignment: one sentence describing what role AI played and what role you played.
Activity 4: Write Your AI Use Statement

What students do
Students draft a personal AI use statement for a specific assignment they are currently working on (or a recent one). The statement must address: (1) What AI tool(s) they used, if any. (2) At what stage(s) of the work. (3) What they did themselves that AI did not do. (4) Why they made those choices. Statements are shared in pairs. Partners check each other's statements for vagueness or gaps.

Why it works
Writing a disclosure statement is harder than it sounds. Students discover that vague statements like "I used AI for help" don't actually communicate anything meaningful. Drafting a clear statement requires students to be precise about what they actually did — which builds metacognitive awareness about their own process, independent of the disclosure function.

Debrief question
Did writing the statement change how you thought about what you did? If the statement felt uncomfortable to write, what does that tell you?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "I used [tool] to [specific task] at [stage of work]. I then [what I did with the output]."
  • Keep a brief process log while working on AI-assisted assignments — a few sentences at each stage. It makes the disclosure statement easy to write at the end.

Environmental Responsibility

AI has material costs. Students who understand this are better equipped to make deliberate choices — not to avoid AI, but to use it well.

A note on numbers: Energy estimates for AI systems vary widely depending on the model, query complexity, hardware, and data center efficiency. The figures below reflect current best estimates but carry real uncertainty. Teach the order of magnitude, not the exact number, and model the habit of citing sources and acknowledging uncertainty.
Activity 5: What Does a Prompt Actually Cost?

What students do
Students are introduced to three categories of AI energy cost — training, inference (running queries), and supporting infrastructure (cooling, networking) — with honest framing about what is and isn't well-established. They then examine a comparison table:

Text query (e.g., Gemini)~0.3 watt-hoursMicrowave for ~1 secondModerate — varies by model and query

Large model training run~thousands of MWhLifetime emissions of several carsHigher — documented for specific runs
All data centers globally (2024)~415 TWh/year~1.5% of global electricityHigh — IEA, 2024

Students identify which figures they trust most, which least, and why. Then they discuss: if individual query costs are small and uncertain, why does it still matter how you use AI?

Why it works
Students worried about AI's environmental impact often have the scale wrong in both directions — overestimating individual query cost while underestimating cumulative and training costs. The table with explicit confidence levels models scientific epistemic practice and gives students tools to evaluate future claims. The discussion question reframes individual choice as part of aggregate behavior, which is where the real environmental story lives.

Debrief question
If your individual query is roughly equivalent to a microwave running for one second, why might it still be worth thinking carefully about how you prompt?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Before prompting: ask whether a traditional search, a phone call, or your own memory could accomplish this just as well.
  • Batch related questions into one prompt rather than asking in fragments.
  • Choose a smaller or task-specific model when a large general-purpose model isn't needed.
Activity 6: The Sustainability Scorecard

What students do
Students apply a five-question scorecard to three AI tasks they have completed in the past two weeks: (1) Necessity: Could a non-AI method (search, library, a person) have accomplished this adequately? (2) Model fit: Did I use the smallest, most task-appropriate model available? (3) Prompt efficiency: Did I get there in one or two exchanges, or ten? (4) Output necessity: Did I generate text, images, or video I didn't actually use? (5) Reflection: Would I make the same choice again, knowing what I know now? Students score each task, share one example where their scorecard revealed a habit worth changing, and discuss.

Why it works
The goal is not guilt — it's deliberateness. Students who apply the scorecard discover that most of their AI use holds up fine under scrutiny, and some of it doesn't. That discrimination is the competency being built. Connecting resource use to reflection also sets up the AI use statement from Activity 4 as a natural culminating document.

Debrief question
Which of the five questions was hardest to answer honestly? What would it take to answer it more easily next time?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • The practical rule: use the smallest, simplest, least wasteful AI process that accomplishes the learning or professional goal.
  • Disable ambient AI features (Copilot autofill, AI-assisted search suggestions) when you're not actively using them for a specific purpose.
  • Companion resource: "Using AI Responsibly Means Using It Efficiently: A Guide to Token Conservation" (https://www.notion.so/34e469e3ddfd811ab27ef0bad010246d)

Accountability and Judgment

When AI gets something wrong and a human acted on it, who is responsible? Students need a framework for thinking about this before they need it for real.

Activity 7: The Accountability Chain

What students do
Students are given three scenarios: (1) A student submits a paper citing a source that doesn't exist — AI generated the citation and they didn't check it. (2) A nurse practitioner recommends a dosage based on an AI tool's output that turned out to be wrong for the patient's specific medication combination. (3) A hiring manager screens out a candidate based on an AI resume-ranking tool that was later found to penalize certain zip codes. For each scenario, students identify: Who is responsible? What should have happened differently? At what point in the chain?

Why it works
Accountability for AI errors is distributed across developers, deployers, and users — and students are often in the user role. Working through the chain in low-stakes scenarios builds the habit of asking "what is my responsibility here?" before acting on AI output in higher-stakes contexts. The three scenarios are calibrated from low stakes (student paper) to high (medical, hiring) to show how the same structural question scales.

Debrief question
At what point in each scenario did someone have a realistic opportunity to catch the error? What would have required them to take it?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "What's the worst-case consequence if this answer is wrong? Does that consequence warrant verification before I act on it?"
  • "I'm going to use this output in a context that affects someone else. What do I owe them in terms of checking this?"

Culminating Activity: Your Personal AI Ethics Statement

After working through activities in this unit, students have developed positions on privacy, disclosure, environmental responsibility, and accountability. The culminating activity asks them to write it down.

Task: Draft a personal AI ethics statement — 200 to 300 words — that addresses: (1) What you will and won't share with AI tools, and why. (2) How you will disclose AI use in academic and professional contexts. (3) How you think about resource use and efficiency when prompting. (4) How you will handle situations where AI output affects other people.

Statements are shared in small groups. Groups identify the principle they agree on most and the one where they disagree most.

The statement is a living document — students should expect to revise it as their AI use evolves. The point is not to get it perfect now but to have thought carefully enough to have a genuine starting position.

Assessment Note

Ethical reasoning doesn't show up reliably on multiple choice. For this unit, the most useful assessment artifacts are:

  • The personal AI use statement from Activity 4, evaluated for specificity and honesty rather than length
  • The personal AI ethics statement from the culminating activity, evaluated for whether the student has taken a genuine position — not just listed correct-sounding principles
  • A brief reflection on how the student's position changed, or didn't, across the unit

Avoid assessing whether students reached the "right" ethical conclusions. The competency is the quality of the reasoning, not the destination.
