Scaffolding AI Literacy: AI and Your Learning

Unit 4 of 6 · Tier: Literacy / Fluency

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for teaching students how AI use affects their own learning — when it helps, when it quietly undermines, and how to tell the difference in real time. It is part of the Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for psychology courses and adaptable across disciplines.

Each activity follows the same logic: students experience the cognitive effect first, then develop a personal framework for managing it. The goal is not to discourage AI use — it's to make students deliberate rather than automatic about when and how they use it.

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale, including disciplinary connections where relevant
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases or practices students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

This unit works best after Units 1–3. Students who understand what AI is and how it behaves are better positioned to reason about what it's doing to their cognition than students encountering these ideas cold.

Disciplinary connections for psychology instructors: This unit draws on some of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology and the science of learning. Relevant concepts students will encounter in the psychology curriculum include: desirable difficulties and the generation effect (Bjork & Bjork), retrieval practice and the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke), spacing effects, cognitive load theory (Sweller), the illusion of knowing (Glenberg), and the Google effect on memory (Sparrow et al., 2011). None of these need to be introduced formally in this unit — the activities are designed to produce the experience first. But instructors teaching this in a Cognitive Psychology or Learning & Memory context can make the connections explicit, and students who encounter these concepts later will have a concrete AI-use anchor for each one.

Cognitive Offloading

Cognitive offloading — using an external tool to reduce the mental work you do yourself — is not inherently bad. Calculators, spell-checkers, and GPS are all cognitive offloading, and we don't generally think they're making us worse. But AI offloading is different in scope and timing in ways that matter for learning. These activities make those differences visible.

Activity 1: The Offloading Spectrum

What students do

Students are given a list of 12 AI use scenarios and asked to place each one on a spectrum from "this offloading is fine" to "this offloading is costing me something important."

Example scenarios: (1) Using AI to check spelling in a final draft. (2) Using AI to generate an outline before thinking through the structure yourself. (3) Using AI to explain a concept you've read but don't understand. (4) Using AI to explain a concept instead of reading the original material. (5) Using AI to generate practice questions for an exam. (6) Using AI to check your answers to practice questions you've already attempted. (7) Using AI to write a first draft of a reflection essay. (8) Using AI to give you feedback on a reflection essay you've already written. (9) Using AI to summarize a reading you haven't done. (10) Using AI to summarize a reading you've already done to check your comprehension. (11) Using AI to solve a problem you're stuck on after 10 minutes of trying. (12) Using AI to solve a problem before trying it yourself.

Students sort individually, then compare in pairs. Pairs rarely agree perfectly — the disagreements are the content of the debrief.

Why it works

The list is structured so that nearly identical tasks (11 vs. 12, 9 vs. 10, 6 vs. 7) differ only in sequence — before or after your own attempt. This is the key variable, and most students haven't thought about it explicitly. Sorting the list forces them to articulate what principle they're actually using, which is the goal. Students who leave this activity with the intuition that timing matters more than task type have the core insight of the unit.

Debrief question

What principle did you use to sort? Did you and your partner use the same principle? Where did it break down?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • Before opening AI: "Have I tried this myself first? If not, why not — is it because I genuinely can't start, or because starting feels effortful?"
  • "I've attempted this myself. Here's what I have so far and where I'm stuck. Help me from here."
Activity 2: The Google Effect, Live

What students do

This is a two-round memory activity.

Round 1: Students are given 10 factual statements to read and remember. They are told they will be tested. After reading, they take a 5-minute filler task. Then they write down everything they remember.

Round 2 (different day or later in the same class): Students are given 10 different factual statements. This time, they are told the information is being saved for them and they can access it anytime. After reading, same filler task, same recall test.

The instructor compares recall rates across conditions. Round 2 recall is typically lower — students remember less when they believe the information is externally stored.

Instructor note: This replicates the basic finding of Sparrow, Liu, & Wegner (2011) — "Google Effects on Memory" — adapted for classroom use. You don't need to disclose this until after the activity. The effect is reliable enough to demonstrate in a class setting, though individual variation is high. Frame results as illustrative, not conclusive.

Why it works

Students experience the offloading effect on their own memory rather than reading about it. The finding — that believing information is saved reduces how hard you encode it — is directly applicable to AI use: if you can always ask AI again, your brain has less incentive to consolidate the information the first time. This is not an argument against using AI. It's an argument for knowing that this is happening and deciding deliberately whether it matters for a given learning goal.

Debrief question

If you can always ask AI to re-explain something, does it matter whether you remembered it? When does it matter, and when doesn't it?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • After AI explains something: close the window and write a one-sentence summary in your own words before moving on.
  • "Explain this to me. Then I'm going to close this window and try to explain it back to you from memory. Tell me what I got wrong."

The Timing Problem

The research on desirable difficulties is clear: struggle that precedes instruction produces better long-term retention than instruction that prevents struggle. AI is very good at preventing struggle. These activities make the learning cost of premature help concrete.

Activity 3: Generation vs. Reception

What students do

Students are split into two groups for a learning task on a topic new to both groups.

Group A (Generation): Given a prompt and asked to generate an answer — even a guess — before receiving any instruction. "Based on what you already know, what do you think explains X? Write your best attempt before looking anything up." Then they receive the correct explanation.

Group B (Reception): Given the correct explanation directly, without the generation prompt.

Both groups take the same short retention test 10–15 minutes later, and again (if feasible) at the next class meeting.

The instructor shares the results and introduces the generation effect: attempting to produce an answer before receiving it improves retention, even when the initial attempt is wrong.

Instructor note: This is a classroom demonstration of a robust finding, not a controlled experiment. Results will vary. Frame it as an illustration, share the research basis, and let the data from the class be a starting point for discussion rather than a proof.

Why it works

The generation effect is one of the most replicated findings in memory research, and it maps directly onto AI use: students who ask AI for the answer before attempting it themselves are trading long-term retention for short-term ease. This activity makes that trade-off visceral. Students in Group A who felt the effortfulness of generating before receiving — and then outperform Group B on retention — have experienced the desirable difficulty principle in a way that a lecture about it cannot replicate.

Debrief question

Group A: Did generating a guess first feel like wasted effort, or did it change how you received the explanation? Group B: Did receiving the explanation directly feel efficient? Did that efficiency translate to retention?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Before you explain this to me, I'm going to write my best current understanding. Then tell me what I got right, what I got wrong, and what I missed entirely."
  • "Don't explain this yet. Give me a hint that helps me figure it out myself."
Activity 4: The Illusion of Knowing

What students do

Students read an AI explanation of a moderately complex concept — something from their current coursework. After reading, they rate their understanding on a 1–7 scale. They do NOT take notes or write anything.

Then, without looking back at the AI output, they attempt to explain the concept in writing as if teaching it to a classmate who has never encountered it.

After writing, they compare their explanation to the original AI output and rate: how much of what they thought they understood did they actually retain well enough to re-explain?

Why it works

This demonstrates the illusion of knowing — the well-documented gap between feeling like you understand something and being able to reproduce or apply it. AI explanations are fluent and well-organized, which makes them feel comprehensible in the moment. That feeling is real but unreliable as an indicator of durable learning. Students who discover the gap between their confidence rating and their re-explanation performance have experienced the illusion directly. This is one of the most transferable insights in the unit: it applies to reading textbooks, watching lectures, and any other passive reception of well-organized information.

Debrief question

How large was the gap between your confidence rating and your re-explanation? What would have closed that gap?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • After any AI explanation: attempt to re-explain it in writing without looking before moving on.
  • "I'm going to try to explain this back to you. Correct any errors and fill in what I missed."
  • "Quiz me on this rather than explaining it to me."

Building Your Decision Framework

Rules don't transfer. A student who memorizes "always struggle before asking AI" will apply it badly in novel situations. These activities help students build a personal decision framework — a set of questions they ask themselves — that they can apply flexibly across contexts.

Activity 5: The Decision Tree

What students do

Students are given four academic scenarios and asked to decide, for each one, whether and how they would use AI. They must justify each decision in writing.

Scenarios: (1) You're starting a paper on a topic you know well. You could ask AI to outline it, or you could outline it yourself first. (2) You've been stuck on a statistics problem for 20 minutes and have no idea where to start. (3) You're studying for an exam by re-reading your notes. AI could generate practice questions. (4) You understand the concept but can't find the right words to express it in your essay.

After individual decisions, students share in pairs and look for the implicit principles they used. The instructor then introduces a simple decision framework students can apply to any situation:

Before opening AI, ask:

  1. Is this a task where struggle is the point — where the effort itself builds the skill or knowledge I need?
  2. Have I made a genuine attempt, or am I avoiding the effortful part?
  3. If AI does this for me, what am I no longer practicing?
  4. Will I be able to do this without AI in the context where it matters (exam, job, conversation)?

Why it works

The four questions give students a portable decision procedure rather than a brittle rule. Question 3 is particularly useful: students who ask "what am I no longer practicing?" before using AI are doing metacognitive work that transfers across tasks and courses. The scenarios are chosen to produce genuine disagreement — scenario 2 (stuck after 20 minutes) is a case where most people agree AI is appropriate; scenario 1 (starting a paper) is more contested and more interesting.

Debrief question

Which scenario was hardest to decide? What made it hard — the task itself, or uncertainty about your own learning goals?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Post the four decision questions somewhere visible during study sessions.
  • "I want to use AI for [task]. Before I do: is the struggle itself the point here? What will I no longer be practicing if AI does this?"
Activity 6: When to Close AI

What students do

Students spend 20 minutes on an AI-assisted study or work task of their choice. Every time they are about to ask AI something, they pause and record: (1) What were they about to ask? (2) Why — were they stuck, avoiding effort, or genuinely needing information they couldn't get another way? (3) Did they ask, or did they try themselves first? (4) What happened?

After 20 minutes, students review their log and identify the moments where they would make a different decision in retrospect.

Why it works

Most AI use is automatic rather than deliberate — students open AI the way they might check their phone, without a conscious decision. This activity interrupts that automaticity by inserting a brief reflective pause before each AI interaction. The log reveals patterns students weren't aware of: some students discover they ask AI to relieve discomfort rather than because they're genuinely stuck. Others discover their AI use is well-calibrated and appropriate. Both findings are useful. The goal is deliberateness, not restriction.

Debrief question

How many of your AI interactions in the 20 minutes were automatic rather than deliberate? What would you have lost and what would you have gained by pausing before each one?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • The 5-second rule: before opening AI, take 5 seconds to ask "why am I opening this right now?"
  • Distinguish between "I'm stuck" (appropriate to ask) and "this is effortful" (worth sitting with).

Culminating Activity: Your Learning Contract

After working through the activities in this unit, students have developed a personal understanding of how AI use interacts with their own learning. The culminating activity asks them to formalize it.

Task: Write a personal AI learning contract — one page — that addresses: (1) What you will do before opening AI — your personal pre-AI checklist (can be the four questions from Activity 5, modified to fit your learning style and goals). (2) Three specific situations where you will not use AI — based on what you've learned about your own cognition in this unit. (3) Three specific situations where AI use is appropriate and well-calibrated for you — where the offloading is genuinely worth it. (4) One habit you are committing to building — drawn from the "Now Try This" practices across this unit.

Contracts are shared in small groups. Groups identify: where did people draw the same lines? Where did they differ, and why?

The contract is revisited at the end of the course. Students write a brief update: what held, what didn't, and what they learned about themselves in the process.

Assessment Note

The competency being built is metacognitive — awareness of one's own cognitive processes and deliberate management of them. This is genuinely difficult to assess on a quiz. The most useful artifacts are:

  • The decision log from Activity 6, evaluated for honest self-reflection rather than "correct" decisions
  • The learning contract from the culminating activity, evaluated for specificity and personal grounding — a contract that could apply to any student has not been personalized enough
  • The end-of-course contract update, evaluated for evidence of genuine self-monitoring over time

Avoid penalizing students whose contracts permit substantial AI use, provided they can articulate why that use is appropriate for their learning goals. The competency is the quality of the reasoning, not the restrictiveness of the conclusion.
