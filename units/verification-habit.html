Scaffolding AI Literacy: The Verification Habit

Unit 3 of 6 · Tier: Literacy / Fluency

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for building the verification habit — the practice of checking AI output systematically rather than by feel. It is part of the Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for psychology courses and adaptable across disciplines.

Each activity follows the same logic: students develop a triage framework first, then practice applying it, then make their verification process visible. The goal is not to verify everything — that's paralysis. It's to verify the right things, in the right way, every time.

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale, including disciplinary connections where relevant
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases or practices students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

This unit works best after Unit 1 (What AI Is and Isn't) and Unit 2 (How AI Behaves). Students who understand why AI produces unreliable output are better motivated to build the verification habit than students who are simply told to check their sources.

Disciplinary connections for psychology instructors: The claim types framework in this unit maps directly onto research methods training students receive in Introduction to Research Methods and throughout the psychology curriculum. Empirical claims require primary sources. Operational definitions matter. Correlation does not establish causation. AI output frequently obscures these distinctions — presenting correlational findings as causal, omitting operational definitions, or citing studies that don't say what the summary claims. Students who already know these distinctions from methods training are well-positioned to apply them here. This unit makes that transfer explicit.

Claim Triage

"Verify everything" produces paralysis. "Verify when it feels wrong" is useless — AI feels right even when it's wrong. Students need a triage framework: what kind of claim is this, and how much verification does it warrant?

Activity 1: The Claim Taxonomy

What students do
Students are given a single AI-generated paragraph on a psychology-adjacent topic — something like a summary of research on sleep and academic performance, or the relationship between social media use and anxiety. The paragraph should contain a mix of claim types. Students sort every claim in the paragraph into four categories:

Claim TypeDescriptionExample
DefinitionalDefines a term or concept"Working memory is the cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information."
EmpiricalMakes a factual claim about the world that could be checked against evidence"Students who sleep fewer than six hours perform significantly worse on memory tasks."
CausalClaims one thing causes another"Social media use increases anxiety in adolescents."
EvaluativeMakes a judgment or recommendation"Students should limit screen time before bed."

After sorting, students answer: which category requires the most verification effort, and why? Which is AI most likely to get wrong? The instructor then introduces a simple triage rule: empirical and causal claims always warrant verification; definitional claims usually warrant a quick check; evaluative claims require your own judgment regardless of what AI says.

Why it works
Students who treat all AI claims as equally trustworthy (or equally suspect) waste effort and miss the claims that actually matter. The taxonomy gives them a decision procedure. For psychology students specifically, the causal claim category connects directly to what they learn in research methods — establishing causation requires experimental control, and AI summaries routinely present correlational findings as causal. Catching that specific error is a transferable skill.

Debrief question
Find one causal claim in the paragraph. What kind of evidence would actually establish that causal relationship? Does AI's summary indicate whether that evidence exists?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Label each claim in your response as definitional, empirical, causal, or evaluative. Flag the empirical and causal claims that most need independent verification."
  • "You've made a causal claim here. Is this based on experimental evidence, correlational evidence, or is this an inference? Be specific."
Activity 2: The Stakes Ladder

What students do
Students are given eight scenarios in which someone is acting on AI output. They rank the scenarios from lowest to highest stakes, then answer: at what point on the ladder does verification become non-negotiable? Example scenarios (instructor should adapt): using AI's definition of a term to help understand a reading; using AI-generated practice questions to study for an exam; citing an AI-provided statistic in a class discussion; citing an AI-provided statistic in a written assignment; using AI's summary of a study in a research paper; recommending a mental health resource to a friend based on AI's suggestion; making a health decision based on AI's medical information; using AI-generated information in a professional context that affects other people. Groups compare their ladders and discuss where they drew the "verification required" line and why.

Why it works
Verification effort should scale with consequence. Students who apply the same level of scrutiny to a study definition as to a medical recommendation are miscalibrated in both directions. The stakes ladder also introduces the idea that context changes the appropriate level of trust — the same AI output that's fine for informal discussion may be unacceptable in a formal submission or professional setting. This connects directly to the accountability framework in Unit 5 (Ethics, Privacy and Judgment).

Debrief question
Where did your group disagree about the line? What principle were you actually arguing about?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • Before acting on any AI output: "What's the worst-case consequence if this is wrong? Does that consequence warrant verification before I use it?"
  • "I'm going to use this in a [context]. Given that, what's the appropriate level of verification?"

The Verification Workflow

Knowing a claim needs checking is not the same as knowing how to check it. These activities give students a practical workflow for different claim types.

Activity 3: Source Matching

What students do
Students are given six claims — two definitional, two empirical, two causal — and a list of source types: peer-reviewed journal article (primary source); systematic review or meta-analysis; textbook; government or institutional website (CDC, APA, NIH); reputable news article citing a primary source; Wikipedia; another AI tool. Students match each claim to the most appropriate source type for verification, and explain why. Then they actually attempt to verify one empirical claim and one causal claim using the source they identified.

Why it works
Students often default to Googling everything, which works poorly for empirical and causal claims that require primary sources. This activity makes the source-claim match explicit. For psychology students, this reinforces the research methods hierarchy they are learning elsewhere in the curriculum — primary sources over secondary, peer-reviewed over popular press — and applies it to a new context where the stakes are clear.

Instructor note: The "another AI tool" option is included deliberately. Students sometimes try to verify AI output by asking a different AI. This is worth discussing explicitly: a second model drawing on similar training data is not an independent source.

Debrief question
When you tried to verify your empirical claim, did you find the original source AI was referring to? If not, what did you find instead?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Don't give me a summary of the research. Give me enough information to find the primary source myself: author, journal, approximate year."
  • "What type of source would I need to verify this claim? Point me toward that source rather than summarizing it for me."
Activity 4: The Psychology Claim Audit

What students do
Students ask AI to summarize the research evidence on a psychological topic of their choice — something they have at least some familiarity with from their coursework. They then audit the summary against three specific criteria drawn from their research methods training:

  1. Causation vs. correlation: Does AI present correlational findings as causal? Find one example.
  2. Operational definitions: Does AI specify how key variables were measured, or does it use vague terms? Find one example.
  3. Population generalizability: Does AI specify the population the research was conducted on, or does it present findings as universal? Find one example.

Students document their findings and share the most striking example with the class.

Why it works
This activity applies students' existing research methods knowledge to AI output — making transfer explicit rather than hoping it happens. The three criteria are chosen because they are common AI failure modes in psychology content specifically: AI summaries routinely present correlational findings causally, drop operational definitions, and universalize findings from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) samples. Students who catch these errors in AI output are also reinforcing the critical reading skills they need for primary literature.

Debrief question
Did AI acknowledge any of these limitations itself, or did you have to find them? What does that tell you about relying on AI to flag its own weaknesses in technical domains?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "For each empirical claim in your response, specify: Was this correlational or experimental evidence? What population was studied? How were the key variables operationally defined?"
  • "What limitations of this research does your summary not mention?"

Documentation

Verification you don't record didn't happen — professionally speaking. These activities build the habit of making the verification process visible and reproducible.

Activity 5: The Verification Log

What students do
Students complete a short AI-assisted task — any task involving factual or empirical claims — and simultaneously maintain a verification log using this structure (columns: Claim, Claim type, Verification source, What I found, Verdict — where Verdict is one of Confirmed / Partially confirmed / Contradicted / Unverifiable). Students complete the log for at least three claims from their task. They then reflect: How many claims did they verify that turned out to be accurate? How many were partially right? How many were wrong or unverifiable?

Why it works
The log externalizes the verification process, which serves two functions. First, it makes the habit easier to build — students who have a structure to fill in are more likely to actually check than students who are simply told to verify. Second, it creates an artifact that makes thinking visible — both to the instructor and to the student themselves. The "unverifiable" verdict is important: some AI claims can't be confirmed or denied with available sources, and students need a name for that outcome rather than defaulting to "probably fine."

Debrief question
What was your verdict distribution? What does it tell you about how much of this particular AI output you should have trusted without checking?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Keep a verification log for any AI-assisted work that will be submitted, published, or used in a professional context.
  • The "unverifiable" verdict is a legitimate outcome — flag it rather than silently accepting the claim.
Activity 6: The Audit Trail

What students do
Students take a piece of AI-assisted work they have already completed — from this class or another — and reconstruct the verification trail retroactively. For each factual or empirical claim in the work, they answer: (1) Did I check this at the time? (2) If yes, how? If no, why not? (3) Checking now: is it accurate? Students document their findings honestly. The instructor makes clear this is not a punitive exercise — the goal is diagnostic, not disciplinary.

Why it works
Retrospective auditing is uncomfortable in exactly the right way. Students who discover they submitted claims they never checked — and that some of them were wrong — internalize the stakes of the verification habit more effectively than any hypothetical scenario. The non-punitive framing is essential: students need to feel safe being honest about what they didn't check, or the exercise produces performance rather than reflection.

Debrief question
If you found a claim you submitted without checking that turned out to be wrong: what was the consequence, or what could the consequence have been? What would you do differently?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Before submitting any AI-assisted work, run a 5-minute claim audit: identify every empirical or causal claim and confirm it has a source.
  • "I didn't check this" is not the same as "this is probably fine."

Culminating Activity: The Verified Summary

Students select a psychological topic they are currently studying or recently studied. They ask AI to summarize the research evidence on that topic, then produce a verified summary — a revised version of the AI output that meets the following criteria:

  1. Every empirical and causal claim is either confirmed against a primary source or flagged as unverified
  2. Correlational findings are described as correlational
  3. Operational definitions are included for key variables where available
  4. Population limitations are noted where relevant
  5. A brief verification log is attached documenting what was checked and what was found

The verified summary is compared to the original AI output. Students write a one-paragraph reflection: what changed, what stayed the same, and what they would now do differently when using AI for research tasks.

Assessment Note

The competency being built is a workflow, not a set of facts. Avoid assessing whether students can define "empirical claim" or recite the source hierarchy. Instead, assess whether they can apply the framework:

  • Given a new AI output, can they correctly identify which claims warrant verification and why?
  • Given a claim, can they identify the appropriate source type and attempt to find it?
  • Does their verification log show genuine checking, or surface-level confirmation bias (only checking claims they expected to be right)?

The verified summary from the culminating activity is the most direct assessment artifact. A student whose verified summary is substantially identical to the original AI output — with no corrections, no flags, no population caveats — has not yet developed the verification habit this unit is building.
