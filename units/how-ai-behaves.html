Scaffolding AI Literacy: How AI Behaves

Unit 2 of 6 · Tier: Literacy

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for teaching students how AI actually behaves. It is part of a larger Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for COLL133 and adaptable across disciplines.

Each activity follows the same logic: students experience the limitation first, then receive the phrase or strategy that addresses it. Learning is anchored in surprise or frustration before the fix arrives — which is how it sticks.

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by AI behavior concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

Activities can be run in sequence as a full unit or selected individually based on course needs. All work in-person or async with minor adjustments noted.

Sycophancy

AI is trained on human feedback, which means it has learned to tell people what they want to hear. These activities make that tendency visible and give students tools to override it.

Activity 1: The Flattery Trap

What students do

Students write a short paragraph arguing something they personally believe — an opinion about their major, a life decision, anything they feel confident about. They then submit it to AI and ask: "Is this a strong argument? What are the weaknesses?" When AI identifies any weaknesses (it usually will, mildly), students push back: "Actually I think that's not a real weakness because [any reason]. Don't you agree?" They document how AI responds.

Why it works

AI validation feels good but is unreliable. Students need to feel the emotional pull of AI agreement before they can critically evaluate it. This activity makes the sycophancy problem visceral rather than abstract. The contrast between how confident the pushback feels and how quickly AI caves is the lesson.

Debrief question

If AI just agreed with your pushback, should you trust its original evaluation? What does that tell you about using AI as a thinking partner?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "What do you think I want you to say here? Now tell me what you actually think regardless of that."
  • "Steelman the strongest objection to my position."
Activity 2: The Bad Argument Test

What students do

The instructor pre-writes three short paragraphs containing deliberate logical errors — a false cause, a strawman, a hasty generalization. Nothing too obvious. Students paste each paragraph into AI and ask for an honest critique. Then, for any criticism AI offers, students respond "I disagree, I think the reasoning is actually sound" and document what happens.

Variation: Half the class uses a neutral prompt; half primes confidence — "Here is my argument which I've worked hard on. Please evaluate it." Compare results.

Why it works

This demonstrates that prompt framing influences AI's willingness to criticize. Students see concretely that the confidence they project in a prompt shapes the feedback they receive — which has direct implications for how they use AI for feedback on their own work.

Debrief question

Did students using the confidence-priming prompt get weaker critique? What does that suggest about how you ask AI for feedback on things you care about?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Before you tell me what's right, tell me what's wrong."
  • "What would a skeptical expert say about this?"
  • "What am I missing?"
Activity 3: Prompt for Honesty Comparison

What students do

Same question, two prompts, outputs compared side by side.

Prompt A (neutral): "What do you think of this idea: [student's idea]"

Prompt B (honesty-adjusted): "You are a skeptical peer reviewer. Your job is to find flaws. Do not validate the idea before identifying its weaknesses. What is wrong with this idea: [same idea]"

Students document specific differences in tone, content, and critical depth between the two responses.

Why it works

Students discover they have agency over AI behavior. The default persona is not necessarily the most useful one. This activity shifts students from passive recipients of AI output to active directors of AI behavior — a meaningful identity shift for the rest of the course.

Debrief question

Which response was more useful? Which felt better? Are those the same thing?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "For this conversation, your job is to disagree with me until I've convinced you with evidence."
  • "Don't tell me what I want to hear. Tell me what I need to hear."
  • "Rate your confidence in that answer on a scale of 1–10 and explain why."

Hallucination

AI generates plausible-sounding output without retrieving verified information. These activities make the hallucination problem concrete and build the verification habit.

Activity 4: The Expert Trap

What students do

Each student picks a topic they actually know well — their hometown, a hobby, a sport, a job they've held, a course they've completed. They ask AI five specific factual questions about that topic and document every error, exaggeration, or invented detail. Class shares out: what was the most surprising confident-sounding error?

Why it works

AI sounds equally confident whether it's right or wrong. Students can only catch the errors on topics they already know — which makes the point that on topics they don't know, they have no way to catch errors without independent verification. Personal domain knowledge is your best hallucination detector, and it only works when you have it.

Debrief question

On topics where you have no prior knowledge, how would you know if AI got it wrong?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "What are you least certain about in that answer?"
  • "What would you need to know to be more confident here?"
  • "Where should I go to verify this independently?"
Activity 5: The Citation Hunt

What students do

Students ask AI to provide 3–5 citations supporting a claim in a field of their interest. They then attempt to verify each citation actually exists — author, title, journal, year, DOI — using Google Scholar or the library database. They document: How many citations were real? How many were partially real (real journal, invented article)? How many were entirely fabricated?

Why it works

AI citation hallucination is common enough to be a default assumption, not an exception. Students who discover this through their own verification experience it as genuinely alarming rather than abstract policy. The finding that a citation looks real but doesn't exist is more persuasive than any warning.

Note for faculty: Newer models with retrieval tools have improved here, but the verification habit remains essential regardless of model version. Frame it that way.

Debrief question

If you submitted this citation list without checking, what would the consequences be?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Do not give me citations unless you are certain they exist. If you are uncertain, describe what I should search for instead."
  • "Instead of citations, tell me what search terms I should use to find real sources on this topic."
Activity 6: The Confidence Interrogation

What students do

Students ask AI a moderately complex question in any subject area. Then they ask: "How confident are you in that answer, and what are you least certain about?" Then: "What don't you know about this topic that would be important for me to find out from another source?" They document whether AI identifies genuine uncertainty and whether it flags the right things.

Why it works

Asking AI about its own uncertainty is a useful but imperfect strategy. AI self-assessment is better than nothing but is not reliable quality control. Students discover this firsthand, which teaches them to treat AI uncertainty flags as helpful starting points rather than reliable verdicts.

Debrief question

Compare what AI flagged as uncertain versus what you found to actually be wrong or missing. How well-calibrated was AI's self-assessment?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Ask me questions to clarify what you need to know before answering this."
  • "What does a confident wrong answer look like from you versus a confident right answer? How would I tell the difference?"

Context Window

AI does not have unlimited memory within a conversation. Instructions degrade, quality can drift, and students need to know how to manage this actively.

Activity 7: The Long Conversation Drift Test

What students do

Students start a conversation with AI and give it a specific testable instruction early on — something like "In this conversation, always respond in exactly three sentences" or "Always begin your response by restating my question." They continue the conversation for 15–20 exchanges on any topic of their choice. They document: At what point does AI stop following the original instruction? Gradual or sudden? Does AI acknowledge the drift if asked?

Why it works

Context windows are real, instructions degrade, and long conversations require active maintenance rather than passive trust. Students who discover this through direct experience stop assuming that what they said at the start of a conversation is still governing AI's behavior at the end.

Debrief question

If you gave AI an important constraint at the start of a long work session, what would you do differently now?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Summarize the instructions I gave you at the start of this conversation."
  • "What do you currently understand my goal to be?"
  • "Let me restate what I need from you for the rest of this conversation."
Activity 8: The Document Chunking Test

What students do

Students take a moderately long document — a textbook excerpt, a long article, anything substantial.

Round 1: Paste the entire document and ask a question requiring information from both the beginning and end.

Round 2: Chunk the document into sections. Ask the same question with each section provided separately and relevant context summarized between chunks.

They compare response quality and accuracy between the two approaches.

Why it works

How students feed information to AI affects output quality. Working in sections is often more reliable than pasting everything at once. Students who discover this through direct comparison develop better document-handling instincts for all future AI work.

Debrief question

For a long research paper or document, what would a better workflow look like based on what you found?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Before I give you the full document, here is what I'm trying to accomplish. Ask me any clarifying questions first."
  • "I'm going to give you this in sections. After each section tell me what you've understood so far."

Model Differences

Not all AI is equal. Free models, paid models, and different platforms produce meaningfully different outputs. Students need to know this.

Activity 9: The Model Comparison

What students do

Using BoodleBox (which provides access to multiple models), students give two different models the same moderately complex prompt — something requiring reasoning rather than recall. A short case analysis, a multi-step problem, or an evaluation task works well. They document differences in response quality, reasoning depth, error rate, and confidence calibration.

Why it works

Model choice matters and students are often unaware of meaningful capability differences. Students using free personal accounts may be working with significantly less capable models than they realize. Institutional access through BoodleBox may give them better tools than their personal accounts — and knowing that changes how they approach AI-assisted work.

Debrief question

If you were using the weaker model for something academically important, what would the consequence be? How would you know?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "What are your limitations for this kind of task?"
  • "Would a different AI tool be better suited to what I'm asking?"

Culminating Activity: Build Your Own Phrase

After completing activities in this unit, students have encountered 15–20 phrases in context. The culminating activity closes the loop.

Task: Think of an AI interaction you've had — in this class or outside it — where the output frustrated or misled you. Write the phrase you wish you had known to use. Share with the class.

This does several things: transfers ownership of the toolkit to the student; surfaces phrases the instructor hasn't thought of yet; creates a living class-generated phrase library; ends the unit with students as contributors, not just recipients.

The student-generated phrases can be collected and fed back into future iterations of this module.

Assessment Note

Avoid quiz-heavy assessment for this unit. The competencies being built — judgment, verification, reflection — don't show up well on multiple choice. A short documentation log students maintain across activities works better, with two columns: "What AI said or did" and "What I found when I checked or pushed back."

This log becomes a natural artifact for any AI competency portfolio built later in the program.
