Scaffolding AI Literacy: What AI Is and Isn't

Unit 1 of 6 · Tier: Literacy

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for teaching students what AI language models actually are — how they work, why they produce fluent-sounding output, and what that means for how to use them well. It is part of the Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for psychology courses and adaptable across disciplines.

Each activity follows the same logic: students build an accurate mental model first, then test it against their existing assumptions. The goal is not to make students skeptical of AI — it's to replace vague intuitions with a framework that makes every subsequent unit more coherent.

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale, including disciplinary connections where relevant
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases or practices students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

This unit works best as the first in the sequence. Units 2 onward assume students have the mental model established here.

Disciplinary connections for psychology instructors: This unit builds on students' existing knowledge of neurons and synaptic transmission from General Psychology — the idea of weighted connections strengthened by exposure is the right intuition and most students already have it. The connectionist architecture introduced here is treated formally in Cognitive Psychology (PDP models, pattern completion, predictive processing) and Biological Psychology (Hebbian learning, long-term potentiation). You can frame this unit as a preview: "You'll see this family of ideas again, in more depth, in Cog and Biopsych. For now, we're building just enough of the model to understand why AI behaves the way it does."

What Is It, Actually?

Before students can reason about AI behavior, they need a mental model of what the system is doing. These activities replace vague intuitions — "it's intelligent," "it's just autocomplete" — with something more accurate and more useful.

Activity 1: The Next Word Game

What students do

The instructor reads a series of sentence fragments aloud. Students write down the first word that comes to mind to complete each one. Examples:

  • "The doctor told the patient to take two..."
  • "She opened the door and saw a..."
  • "The experiment failed because the researchers forgot to..."
  • "After years of practice, he finally..."

Students share their completions. The class notes: how much agreement is there? Where does it break down? Then the instructor reveals that this is exactly the core task an AI language model is trained to do — predict the most likely next token given everything that came before — just at massive scale, across billions of examples, with hundreds of billions of weighted parameters.

The instructor then asks: if that's the mechanism, what does it mean when AI sounds confident? What does it mean when it's wrong?

Why it works

This activity grounds the abstract in something students just did with their own cognition. The completions tend to converge strongly on high-frequency continuations ("pills," "stranger," "control") and diverge on lower-frequency ones — which maps directly onto how language models behave. High-frequency patterns produce confident, consistent output. Lower-frequency or more specific queries produce more variable, less reliable output.

For psychology students specifically: this connects naturally to spreading activation models of memory and prototype effects in categorization — both of which involve pattern completion over weighted associations. You don't need to make this connection explicit in COLL133, but it's available for instructors teaching this in a Cognitive Psychology context.

Debrief question

You just did next-word prediction using your own memory and experience. Where did you agree with your classmates? Where did you diverge? What does that tell you about what AI is likely to get right versus wrong?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "This is a high-stakes or specific claim. What's your confidence level, and what would I need to verify independently?"
  • "Give me the less obvious completion — not the most predictable answer, but a well-reasoned alternative."
Activity 2: Where Did It Learn That?

What students do

Students are given four prompts to try in any AI tool:

  1. Ask about a well-documented historical event
  2. Ask about something very recent (within the past few months)
  3. Ask about something highly specific to their local context (their town, their campus, a local business)
  4. Ask about themselves

For each, they document: How confident did AI sound? How accurate was it? Did it acknowledge any limitations?

Then the class builds a shared map: what kinds of things does AI know well, and why? What kinds of things does it not know, and why?

The instructor introduces the concept of training data and knowledge cutoffs — not as a technical deep-dive, but as the answer to the question students just generated: the model knows what was in its training data, and nothing else.

Why it works

Students frequently anthropomorphize AI — treating it as if it has access to the world the way a person does. This activity makes the boundary between training data and real-time knowledge concrete and personal. The "ask about yourself" prompt is particularly effective: AI either knows nothing, confabulates, or (for public figures) produces information that may be outdated or wrong. The gap between how the model sounds and what it actually has access to is the lesson.

Debrief question

AI sounded equally confident across all four prompts. What does that tell you about using confidence as a signal of accuracy?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Is this something that could have changed since your training data? If so, tell me what to search for to verify it."
  • "What would you need access to that you don't currently have in order to answer this reliably?"

Why It Sounds So Confident

Fluency is a feature of how language models are built, not a signal of accuracy. These activities make that distinction visceral rather than abstract.

Activity 3: The Fluency Illusion

What students do

Students are given four short paragraphs — printed, not on screen — and asked to rate each one on two dimensions: (1) How well-written does this seem? (1–7 scale) (2) How accurate does this seem? (1–7 scale)

Two of the paragraphs are factually accurate. Two contain subtle but significant errors. All four are written in polished, confident prose. Students rate individually, then share.

After sharing, the instructor reveals which paragraphs contained errors. The class examines: did writing quality predict accuracy? Did students' confidence ratings track actual accuracy?

Instructor note: Generate the paragraphs fresh using an AI tool before class — ask it to write two accurate and two subtly wrong paragraphs on a topic relevant to your course. This keeps the content current and avoids the paragraphs circulating online.

Why it works

This is a direct demonstration of the fluency heuristic — the well-documented tendency to judge fluently presented information as more true. Psychology students will recognize this from their coursework; this activity shows it operating on AI output specifically. The finding that writing quality and accuracy are decoupled in AI output is the conceptual anchor for every verification activity that follows in Unit 3.

For instructors teaching this in a Social Psychology or Cognitive Psychology context: this connects directly to processing fluency research (Reber, Schwarz, & Winkielman, 2004) and the illusory truth effect. You can make that connection explicit or leave it implicit depending on course level.

Debrief question

You just experienced the fluency heuristic on AI output. Knowing this, what habit would help you catch the well-written wrong answer?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "You've given me a fluent answer. Now tell me specifically what you're least certain about in it."
  • "What's the strongest argument that this answer is wrong?"
Activity 4: Confident and Wrong — A Taxonomy

What students do

Working in pairs, students attempt to get AI to produce confident-sounding wrong answers across four categories: (1) A fabricated citation — ask for sources on a real topic. (2) A false historical detail — ask about a real event with a specific numerical claim (dates, statistics, counts). (3) A plausible-but-wrong causal claim — ask why something happens in a domain neither student knows well. (4) An outdated fact — ask about something that was true before the training cutoff but may have changed.

Pairs document their best example from each category: the prompt, the AI output, and how they verified the error.

Class shares findings. Instructor maps the categories onto a simple framework: fabrication (pattern completion with no factual anchor), compression error (real information stored imprecisely), cutoff error (accurate at training time, no longer true).

Why it works

Naming the failure modes gives students a taxonomy they can apply going forward. "That sounds like a fabrication" and "that might be a cutoff error" are more useful than the generic "AI makes mistakes." The activity also gives students genuine agency — they're actively probing the system rather than passively receiving warnings about it.

Debrief question

Which category of error was easiest to get AI to produce? Which was hardest to catch? What does that asymmetry tell you about where to focus your verification effort?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "Is this claim something that could be a compression error — approximately right but imprecise on the specifics?"
  • "Flag anything in your answer that falls into one of these categories: fabricated, imprecisely remembered, or potentially outdated."

What It Can and Can't Do

Students need a calibrated map of AI capability — not "AI is amazing" or "AI can't be trusted" but a usable sense of where it adds genuine value and where it doesn't.

Activity 5: The Capability Map

What students do

Students are given a list of 20 tasks and asked to sort them into three categories: AI does this well (reliable enough to use with light verification), AI does this partially (useful starting point but requires significant checking or editing), AI does this poorly (not worth using AI for, or actively risky).

Example tasks (instructor should adapt to course context): summarize a long article I paste in; find me the most recent study on this topic; explain a concept I already understand to check my understanding; give me feedback on whether my argument is logically sound; tell me the current price of a stock; generate practice exam questions on a topic; cite sources for a claim; help me brainstorm counterarguments; translate a passage from Spanish; tell me whether a specific medication is safe for a specific patient; write a first draft of a routine email; identify the author's emotional tone in a passage I paste; tell me what happened in the news today; help me debug why my argument isn't persuasive; give me accurate statistics about a social issue.

Students sort individually, then compare in groups. Groups rarely agree perfectly — and that disagreement is the content of the debrief.

Why it works

The goal is not to produce the "correct" capability map — it's to surface the reasoning students are using. Some students sort based on experience ("I've seen it fail at this"), some based on the mental model from earlier activities ("this requires real-time data, so..."), some based on intuition. Making those different reasoning strategies visible helps students calibrate future judgments. The capability map also becomes a practical reference: students can return to it and revise it as their AI experience grows.

Debrief question

Where did your group disagree most? Was the disagreement about AI's capability, or about how much verification effort is worth it for that task?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • Before starting any AI-assisted task: "Is this a task where AI is likely to be reliable, partially reliable, or unreliable? What does that mean for how I use the output?"
  • "What category of task is this, and what's the appropriate level of trust for that category?"
Activity 6: The Human-AI Comparison

What students do

Students complete the same task two ways — first without AI, then with AI assistance — and compare the results.

The task should be one where both approaches are plausible and neither is obviously superior. Good options: write a one-paragraph explanation of a concept covered in class this week; generate five exam practice questions on a topic you just studied; identify the three strongest objections to an argument you've been given.

Students document: Where did AI add value? Where did your own version hold up better? What did you know that AI didn't? What did AI do that you couldn't or wouldn't have done as quickly?

Why it works

This reframes the question from "is AI good or bad" to "what is the right division of labor here?" Students who discover that their own explanation was more accurate, or that AI's practice questions were too easy, or that AI missed the most important objection — those students leave with a calibrated rather than credulous relationship with the tool. Students who discover AI genuinely helped them think faster or more broadly also leave with something real: they've identified a use case that works for them.

Debrief question

Based on this comparison, what's one thing you should probably keep doing yourself, and one thing where AI assistance is genuinely worth it?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "I've drafted my own version of this. Here it is. Where does your version add something mine doesn't, and where does mine hold up better?"
  • "What can you do here that I probably can't do as quickly myself? What should I still do myself?"

Culminating Activity: Revise Your Mental Model

At the start of Unit 1, before any activities, students write a brief response (3–5 sentences) to this prompt: "What do you think an AI language model actually is and how does it work? Be as specific as you can, but don't worry about being right — just write what you currently think."

These are collected (or saved by students) and returned at the end of the unit.

Task: Students re-read their original response and write a revision. The revision must:

  1. Identify one thing they had right
  2. Identify one thing they had wrong or incomplete
  3. Write a new 3–5 sentence description that reflects what they now understand

Revisions are shared in small groups. Groups identify the most common misconception they all started with.

This before/after structure does several things: makes conceptual change visible to students (which is motivating); gives the instructor diagnostic information about incoming mental models; establishes the norm that updating your understanding based on evidence is the goal — which is the epistemological stance the whole curriculum is trying to build.

Assessment Note

The competency being built in this unit is a working mental model, not factual recall. Avoid asking students to recite definitions of tokens, parameters, or training data. Instead, assess whether students can apply the model:

  • Given a new AI failure, can they identify which category it belongs to (fabrication, compression error, cutoff error)?
  • Given a new task, can they make a reasonable prediction about where AI will be reliable versus unreliable?
  • Has their stated mental model changed from the beginning to the end of the unit in ways that reflect the activities?

The revised mental model from the culminating activity is the most direct assessment artifact. A student who ends the unit still describing AI as "basically intelligent" or "just autocomplete" has not yet developed the working model this curriculum requires.
