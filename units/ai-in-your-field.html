Scaffolding AI Literacy: AI in Your Field and Your Future

Unit 6 of 6 (capstone) · Tier: Fluency

This page documents hands-on classroom activities for helping students connect AI literacy to their own career trajectories and professional futures. It is part of the Scaffolding AI Literacy series designed for psychology courses and adaptable across disciplines.

This is the capstone unit. It assumes students have worked through Units 1–5 and have developed a working mental model of AI, a verification habit, a sense of AI's ethical dimensions, and an understanding of how AI affects their own learning. This unit asks them to look outward: what does AI mean for the fields they are entering, and what kind of professional relationship with AI do they want to have?

How to Use This Page

Activities are grouped by concept. Each toggle contains:

  • The setup — what students do
  • Why it works — the pedagogical rationale, including disciplinary connections where relevant
  • Now Try This — the prompt phrases or practices students earn through the activity
  • Debrief question — to anchor the learning in discussion

This unit works best as the final unit in the sequence. Several activities ask students to draw on artifacts from earlier units — the capability map from Unit 1, the learning contract from Unit 4, and the personal AI ethics statement from Unit 5. Note where those are referenced so students can retrieve them.

A note on epistemic honesty for instructors: The labor market projections for AI's impact on psychology careers are genuinely uncertain. Specific percentages circulate widely in career-guide content but are often untraceable to primary sources. What is well-supported: AI is currently being adopted primarily for administrative and documentation tasks in psychology practice (APA Practitioner Pulse Survey, 2024); the APA released its first formal ethical guidance on AI in professional practice in June 2025; and I-O psychology professional organizations are actively calling for AI literacy in graduate training (SIOP, 2025). Teach the direction of change with confidence. Teach specific numbers with appropriate uncertainty.

Mapping AI to Your Field

AI's impact on psychology is uneven across subfields and career paths. Students who map it to their actual interests leave with something more useful than a generic picture.

Activity 1: The Subfield Map

What students do
Students are given a matrix with psychology subfields across the top (Clinical/Counseling, I-O Psychology, Research/Academic, Neuropsychology/Assessment, Human Factors/UX) and four task categories down the side (Administrative tasks — documentation, scheduling, billing; Assessment and data analysis; Direct human interaction — therapeutic relationship, interviews, supervision; Knowledge work — literature review, report writing, hypothesis generation). Working in small groups (each group assigned one or two subfields), they research and fill in the matrix. For each cell, groups answer: Is AI currently being used here? If so, how? What tasks remain distinctly human, and why? Groups share findings. The class builds a composite picture.

Instructor note: Seed the research with credible current sources. The APA's 2025 Ethical Guidance on AI in Professional Practice and the SIOP's 2025 member survey on generative AI use are good anchors. Career-guide websites with specific percentages should be treated skeptically — model that skepticism explicitly.

Why it works
Students in an introductory psychology course are often unsure which subfield they're heading toward. The matrix format lets them research the subfields they care about rather than receiving a generic picture. It also makes the pattern visible: AI is currently landing hardest on administrative and documentation tasks across almost every subfield, while direct human interaction remains the most stable. That pattern is more instructive than any specific projection. For students interested in clinical or counseling careers specifically: the APA's 2024 Practitioner Pulse Survey found about 1 in 10 psychologists currently use AI at least monthly, primarily for note-taking and administrative work. Most practitioners report using AI to help write emails, develop homework assignments, assist with report writing, and transcribe session notes — not to conduct therapy or make diagnostic decisions.

Debrief question
Across subfields, where is AI landing first? What do those tasks have in common? What does that tell you about where to focus your own skill development?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "I'm interested in [subfield]. What administrative or documentation tasks in that field are currently being supported by AI tools? What does a practitioner still need to do themselves?"
  • "What AI literacy skills does [professional organization, e.g., APA, SIOP] currently recommend for practitioners in this field?"
Activity 2: The Task Autopsy

What students do
Students choose a psychology-adjacent job they are genuinely interested in — school psychologist, UX researcher, HR consultant, clinical case manager, behavioral analyst, research coordinator, or any other. They find a real job posting for that role and perform a task autopsy: (1) List every task mentioned in the job description. (2) Categorize each task: routine and structured / knowledge-intensive / relationship-dependent / judgment-intensive. (3) For each category, assess: is AI currently capable of supporting, replacing, or augmenting this task? (4) Identify the two or three tasks that seem most stable against automation, and explain why. Students share their autopsies and look for cross-cutting patterns.

Why it works
Job descriptions are a more honest data source than labor market projections. They reflect what employers actually ask for right now, and they force students to reason about specific tasks rather than abstract categories. The autopsy framing — working backwards from the job to the task to the automation question — builds the analytical habit of asking "what specifically does this role require?" rather than "will this job exist?" Students consistently find that the tasks most central to the professional identity of psychologists — building therapeutic alliance, ethical judgment, nuanced human interpretation, research design — are the ones AI handles least well. That finding, arrived at through their own analysis, is more credible to them than being told it.

Debrief question
What tasks in your chosen role are most stable against automation? What makes them stable — is it technical difficulty for AI, or something else?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • When evaluating any career claim about AI automation: ask whether it's about the job (too broad) or specific tasks within the job (more useful).
  • "Here is a job description. Which tasks in this role are currently being augmented by AI tools? Which are not, and why?"

The Durable Skills

The skills that make a psychologist valuable — assessment, ethical judgment, therapeutic relationship, research design, nuanced human interpretation — are not what current AI does well. But articulating why requires more than saying "AI can't do empathy." These activities help students build a more precise account.

Activity 3: What AI Can't Do Here — and Why

What students do
Students are given four scenarios in which an AI tool attempts a task central to psychology practice: (1) An AI tool reviews a client's intake questionnaire and generates a preliminary diagnostic impression. (2) An AI tool analyzes transcripts of therapy sessions and flags moments of therapeutic rupture. (3) An AI tool generates a research hypothesis based on a literature review it has summarized. (4) An AI tool produces a neuropsychological assessment report from test scores. For each scenario, students answer: What is AI doing well here? What is it missing, and why? What would a human psychologist bring to this task that the AI output doesn't capture? What are the risks if the human defers to the AI output without critical evaluation? Students share their analyses. The instructor draws out the cross-cutting theme: AI can process information and generate plausible-sounding outputs, but the professional judgment that makes those outputs safe and appropriate requires contextual knowledge, ethical accountability, and interpretive skill that AI doesn't have.

Why it works
This is a direct application of Unit 1's mental model — AI produces fluent, plausible output, not necessarily accurate or contextually appropriate output — to professional practice scenarios. Students who have internalized Unit 1 will recognize the failure mode immediately. Students who haven't will encounter it here in a high-stakes context that makes the stakes clear. The four scenarios are calibrated to cover the range from lower-stakes (hypothesis generation) to higher-stakes (diagnostic impression, neuropsychological report) to show how the same structural limitation scales.

Debrief question
In which scenario was AI most plausible-sounding but most professionally dangerous? What made it dangerous — the error itself, or the confidence with which it was presented?

Now Try This — phrases earned through this activity

  • "You've generated a [clinical/research/assessment] output. What information did you not have access to that a human professional would use to evaluate this? What are the risks of acting on this without that context?"
  • "What would a licensed [psychologist / researcher / assessor] check before using this output professionally?"
Activity 4: The Skills Audit

What students do
Students return to the capability map they built in Unit 1 (Activity 5) and the learning contract from Unit 4. They now reframe both documents through a professional lens: (1) From the capability map: which of the tasks AI does poorly map onto the core competencies of the psychology subfield they're most interested in? (2) From the learning contract: which of the habits they committed to building — struggling before asking AI, verifying before acting, closing AI during retrieval practice — are directly relevant to professional practice in their field? (3) New question: what skills do they currently have, or are developing, that are difficult for AI to replicate in their intended career? Students write a one-page skills audit that answers all three questions. Audits are shared in pairs.

Why it works
This activity makes the curriculum's internal coherence visible to students. The mental model from Unit 1, the verification habit from Unit 3, the learning contract from Unit 4 — these aren't disconnected modules, they're a preparation for professional practice in a field where AI is present but human judgment remains essential. Students who can articulate the connection between their learning habits and their professional durability are better positioned to maintain those habits after the course ends.

Debrief question
Which skill in your audit feels most developed right now? Which feels most important to keep building? Are those the same skill?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Frame AI fluency not as a separate skill but as a multiplier on the human skills that remain essential in your field.
  • "I'm developing skills in [area]. How is AI currently being used in that area, and what does a human still need to bring?"

Your Professional Relationship with AI

Students leave this curriculum having built a toolkit. This section asks them to articulate the philosophy behind it — not just what they do, but why, and what kind of professional they are trying to become.

Activity 5: The Professional AI Use Statement

What students do
Students draft a professional AI use statement — 300 to 400 words — framed not for a course assignment but for a professional context: a job application, a graduate school personal statement, or a professional portfolio. The statement must address: (1) How they currently use AI in their academic and professional work. (2) What they do not use AI for, and why. (3) How they verify AI output before acting on it in consequential contexts. (4) How their approach reflects the ethical standards of their intended field (students should reference at least one real professional guideline — APA Ethics Code, APA's 2025 AI guidance, SIOP guidelines, or equivalent). (5) How they expect their approach to evolve as the technology and the field's norms evolve. Statements are shared in small groups. Groups give each other one piece of feedback: is the statement specific enough to be credible, or does it read like generic AI-literacy talking points?

Why it works
Framing the statement for a professional context rather than a classroom one changes how students write it. Generic statements — "I use AI responsibly and always verify" — don't hold up when the audience is an employer or graduate admissions committee. The peer feedback prompt ("is this specific enough to be credible?") gives students a concrete criterion that maps onto the verification skills they've built across the curriculum. The requirement to reference a real professional guideline also sends students to primary sources — including the APA's June 2025 Ethical Guidance on AI in Professional Practice, which is the most current professional standard available.

Debrief question
If an employer or graduate program asked you to describe your approach to AI in your work, is your statement specific enough to be convincing? What would make it more specific?

Now Try This — practices earned through this activity

  • Keep your professional AI use statement as a living document. Revise it once per semester as your practice and the field's norms evolve.
  • Before any professional AI use: ask whether your approach is consistent with the statement you've written. If not, either change the approach or update the statement — but do one deliberately.

Culminating Activity: The Portfolio Synthesis

This is the capstone for the entire curriculum, not just Unit 6. Students assemble a brief AI literacy portfolio — not a collection of assignments, but a curated argument that they have developed genuine competency across the curriculum. The portfolio contains:

  1. Revised mental model (from Unit 1 culminating activity) — with a one-sentence note on what changed
  2. One verification log entry (from Unit 3) — chosen as the best example of the habit in practice
  3. Personal AI ethics statement (from Unit 5) — revised if needed in light of Unit 6
  4. Skills audit (from Unit 6, Activity 4)
  5. Professional AI use statement (from Unit 6, Activity 5)
  6. One-page reflection — answering: what did you think AI was when this curriculum started? What do you think now? What habit are you most confident you'll carry forward, and why?

The portfolio is not assessed on whether students arrived at the "correct" position on any question. It is assessed on evidence of genuine development: did the student's thinking change, deepen, or become more specific across the curriculum? Does the professional AI use statement reflect the mental model and habits built earlier? Is the reflection honest rather than performative?

Assessment Note

The competency being assessed in this unit — and in the portfolio — is integration and transferability. The questions to ask when evaluating portfolio submissions:

  • Does the professional AI use statement reflect the specific mental model, verification habits, and ethical commitments built across the curriculum, or does it read as if it could have been written on day one?
  • Does the skills audit show genuine engagement with the student's actual career interests, or is it generic?
  • Does the reflection show evidence of intellectual honesty — including places where the student is still uncertain or still working something out?

A portfolio that is polished but generic has not demonstrated the competency. A portfolio that is specific, honest, and shows genuine development — even if some conclusions are tentative — has.
