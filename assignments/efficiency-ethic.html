Why Use This Assignment?

Many students and faculty know that AI has environmental costs, but they often translate that concern into a simple yes/no question: should I use AI or avoid it? That framing misses an important skill. Responsible AI use is not just about whether AI is used — it is also about how much AI is used, whether AI is the right tool, and whether the workflow preserves learning or creates avoidable output and correction.

This assignment makes workflow scale visible. Students complete the same psychology task using two workflows: one unplanned and iterative, one planned and resource-aware. They compare the number of exchanges, approximate output volume, quality of final product, amount of correction required, and what cognitive work they preserved for themselves.

The goal is not to calculate exact carbon emissions. The goal is to develop a professional habit: use the least AI that preserves the learning goal and accomplishes the task well.

The psychology connects directly. Pro-environmental behavior research provides frameworks for understanding how awareness, motivation, context, and perceived control can shape behavior (Steg & Vlek, 2009). Making interaction volume visible gives students something concrete to monitor, but it does not establish the environmental impact of an individual workflow. Effort minimization and satisficing help explain why people choose convenient tools even when another workflow might fit the task better. The rebound effect offers a further caution: efficiency can sometimes enable more total use rather than less. This assignment does not claim that rebound will occur here. It asks students to consider the possibility.

The most responsible AI user is not necessarily the one who avoids it. It is the one who can justify when and how much to use.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Distinguish wasteful, adequate, and resource-aware AI workflows on a concrete task.
  2. Explain why vague prompts and full regenerations can increase unnecessary output and correction.
  3. Compare the quality and learning value of an unplanned workflow versus a resource-aware workflow.
  4. Connect efficient AI use to psychological concepts including effort allocation, cognitive load, and pro-environmental behavior.
  5. Write a transferable personal rule for using AI efficiently without offloading learning.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

Using AI responsibly means using it intentionally. A long, wandering chat can feel productive, but it may produce more output than you need, require more correction, and replace thinking you should have done yourself.

In this assignment, you will complete the same task twice. First, you will use AI the way many people do when they are not planning carefully. Then you will use AI with a resource-aware workflow. You will compare the results — not to prove that one approach is always better, but to make the tradeoffs visible.

Background: What Are You Measuring?

AI tools process text in chunks and generate output based on what you ask. When your prompt is vague, AI must infer what you want, and a poor inference may require correction. When you request a full rewrite instead of a targeted edit, you generate more output than a local change would require. When you re-explain context that already exists in a handoff file, you create duplicate work.

None of this is catastrophic in a single interaction. Across repeated use, however, differences in workflow can accumulate — in output volume, correction burden, the quality of what users produce, and the cognitive work they preserve for themselves. This activity measures those immediate workflow differences. It does not measure energy, water, emissions, or ecological impact directly.

This assignment asks you to observe the small-scale differences you can document, then reason cautiously about what responsible professional practice should look like.

Task

Setup — Choose Your Task

Choose one psychology concept from the current unit. Your instructor may assign the concept or let you choose. Your goal for both workflows is the same: produce a 150 to 200 word explanation of the concept for a classmate who missed the lecture.

The explanation must include a clear definition, one concrete example, one common misunderstanding, and one connection to another course concept.

You must use the same concept and the same output goal in both workflows.

Part 1 — The Unplanned Workflow (15 to 20 minutes)

Open an AI tool and begin with this deliberately loose prompt:

Help me explain [concept] for psychology class.

Continue the conversation until you have a draft you could use. You may ask follow-up questions, request revisions, or ask for a full rewrite.

As you work, keep a log:

MeasureYour count
Number of prompts you sent 
Number of AI responses received 
Approximate total AI output length in words 
Number of full rewrites requested 
Number of targeted edits requested 
Number of factual claims you checked 

Save the final output.

Part 2 — The Resource-Aware Workflow (20 to 25 minutes)

Before opening a new AI chat, write a short plan:

  1. What do I already know about this concept that I do not need AI for?
  2. What part do I actually need help with?
  3. What should AI not do for me?
  4. What source will I use to check accuracy?
  5. What is the smallest useful output I can ask for?

Now open a new chat. Use a planned prompt. You may adapt this model:

I am writing my own 150 to 200 word explanation of [concept] for a psychology class. I want to preserve my own thinking. Ask me one clarifying question if needed. Then give targeted feedback only on accuracy, missing nuance, and one possible misconception to address. Do not rewrite the whole explanation unless I ask.

Use targeted follow-up prompts rather than full rewrites whenever possible.

Keep the same log:

MeasureYour count
Number of prompts you sent 
Number of AI responses received 
Approximate total AI output length in words 
Number of full rewrites requested 
Number of targeted edits requested 
Number of factual claims you checked 

Save the final output.

Part 3 — Compare the Workflows (15 minutes)

Answer each question in three to five sentences.

  1. Which workflow produced the better explanation? Use specific evidence from the outputs.
  2. Which workflow preserved more of your own thinking? How can you tell?
  3. Which workflow used fewer interactions or generated less output? Did lower output mean lower quality?
  4. What, if anything, did the unplanned workflow do that was wasteful, unnecessary, or distracting from the actual learning goal?
  5. What did the resource-aware workflow require you to think through before asking AI? Was that useful?

Part 4 — Write an Efficiency Rule

Write one personal rule for future AI use. It should be specific, transferable, and protect your learning.

Examples:

I will draft my own explanation before asking AI for feedback.
I will ask for targeted edits before asking for a full rewrite.
I will not ask AI to reconstruct context I could have saved in a handoff file.
I will use AI only when it adds value beyond my notes, textbook, search, or peer discussion.

Guiding Questions

  • Did the unplanned workflow feel easier? If so, did that ease come at a cost?
  • What kinds of prompts generated the most unnecessary output?
  • The rebound effect suggests that efficiency gains can sometimes lead to increased total use. Is that a plausible risk in your own AI use? What rule might prevent it?
  • How does resource-aware AI use connect to environmental responsibility without pretending to measure or solve the entire environmental problem?
  • What can individual workflow choices accomplish, and what ecological questions require coordinated institutional or industry action?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Required for Parts 1 and 2. Students may not use AI to write the workflow comparison, the reflection questions, or the personal efficiency rule.

Not allowed: Students may not skip the unplanned workflow, rewrite logs after the fact, or use the same chat for both workflows. Students may not use AI to estimate environmental impact or claim specific carbon savings.

Required disclosure: I used AI twice: once in an unplanned workflow and once in a resource-aware workflow. My comparison answers and efficiency rule are my own work. I am responsible for the final product.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: Any factual claim in the final explanation must be checked against course material or an instructor-approved source.

Resource rule: Use enough AI to complete the comparison, but do not generate extra versions once you have the evidence you need. This assignment studies efficiency — do not turn it into a contest to produce the most output.

Learning protection rule: The resource-aware workflow must begin with your own plan. The point is not to make AI do the planning for you.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • complete both workflows honestly, with accurate logs
  • compare quality and learning value, not just interaction count
  • identify specific waste patterns when the evidence supports them
  • explain how planning changed the interaction
  • avoid overclaiming exact environmental savings or treating output count as a direct ecological measure
  • write a transferable efficiency rule that is specific enough to actually use

Instructor Notes

This assignment does not require faculty to be AI experts. It requires faculty to think carefully about effort allocation and environmental behavior — territory that belongs squarely to psychology. Faculty who are skeptical of AI adoption may be especially prepared to teach this assignment because their concerns are among the questions the activity takes seriously.

On the ecological framing. This assignment deliberately avoids specific claims about carbon savings, per-token emissions, or exact environmental impact. Those estimates vary by infrastructure, model, workload, energy source, and accounting method. A defensible behavioral concern is narrower: resource use that is hard to see can also be harder to monitor and regulate. This assignment makes interaction volume and correction burden visible without treating either as a direct environmental measure. Steg and Vlek (2009) provide a broader framework for pro-environmental behavior; they do not estimate the ecological cost of AI workflows. The goal is to develop a professional habit, not to audit environmental impact.

What the unplanned workflow may reveal. Students often discover that vague prompts generate output requiring correction because they had not clarified what they needed. Other students may find that the unplanned workflow produced output that was better, faster, or more creative than the planned version. Encourage honest reporting. An assignment that rewards evidence over performance of efficiency is doing its job.

The rebound effect is a caution, not a demonstrated result here. Jevons described how efficiency gains in resource use can be accompanied by increased total consumption. That pattern does not prove that a student who becomes more efficient with AI will use more AI. It supplies a question worth asking: could lower friction increase total use enough to offset per-task restraint? Resolution requires intentionality about when to use AI at all, which connects to The Right Tool Audit.

On moral licensing. Research reviewed by Merritt et al. (2010) suggests that prior moral behavior can sometimes license less restrained behavior later. Treat this as a possibility to monitor, not a prediction that students who use AI efficiently today will use it wastefully tomorrow. Efficiency is a professional habit, not a credential.

Addressing the ecological skeptic. Faculty who have avoided AI adoption on ecological grounds may feel this assignment endorses what they have resisted. That concern deserves a direct response rather than dismissal. Personal avoidance is a principled position, and this assignment does not argue against it. Students may nevertheless encounter AI in later coursework and professional settings. Teaching restraint and proportionality — the habit of using only what is needed — is one available educational intervention. The goal is not to convert skeptics but to offer a form of AI instruction aligned with careful, deliberate, and minimally wasteful tool use.

On the efficiency rule. The personal efficiency rule in Part 4 is the most transferable deliverable in the assignment. Discuss these in class — students' rules often reveal different failure modes (over-reliance on full rewrites, re-explaining context, asking for explanations they could look up). A class list of efficiency rules is a reusable artifact.

Provided Workflow Transcripts

Use these if you do not have device access, or if your instructor assigns them instead of running live workflows. Complete Parts 3 and 4 using these transcripts exactly as you would use your own.

Both transcripts use the same task: explain operant conditioning in 150 to 200 words for a classmate who missed the lecture.

Transcript A — Unplanned Workflow

Prompt 1: Help me explain operant conditioning for psychology class.

AI response 1: Operant conditioning is a type of learning where behavior is shaped by its consequences. It was developed by B.F. Skinner, who built on earlier work by Edward Thorndike. The core idea is that behaviors followed by positive consequences tend to increase, while behaviors followed by negative consequences tend to decrease. Skinner identified four main processes: positive reinforcement (adding something desirable), negative reinforcement (removing something aversive), positive punishment (adding something undesirable), and negative punishment (removing something desirable). A classic example is Skinner's experiments with rats in a specially designed box — now called a Skinner box — where the rat learned to press a lever to receive food. Operant conditioning has been applied in education, therapy, parenting, animal training, and workplace motivation. It remains one of the most influential theories in behavioral psychology. (~130 words)

Prompt 2: Can you make it a bit more interesting and add a real-world example?

AI response 2: [Rewrites the entire paragraph with an example about studying for exams. ~160 words. The definition and structure are largely unchanged from response 1; only the example is new.]

Prompt 3: Actually I don't like that example. Can you use something about social media instead?

AI response 3: [Rewrites again with a social media example about likes and posting behavior. ~155 words.]

Prompt 4: That's good but can you add something about common misconceptions?

AI response 4: [Adds a sentence about negative reinforcement being confused with punishment. Rewrites the paragraph again to incorporate it. ~175 words.]

[Student plan written before opening AI:]

  • I need help making the misconception point clear and checking whether my social media example is accurate.

Here is what I have so far: [student draft with definition, social media example, and an attempt at the misconception point]. Give me targeted feedback only: is anything inaccurate, is there a nuance I'm missing, and does my misconception point land clearly? Do not rewrite the whole thing.

AI response 1: Your definition is accurate. One nuance worth adding: negative reinforcement is specifically the removal of an aversive stimulus — many students (and some sources) describe it as simply “bad reinforcement,” which conflates it with punishment. Your social media example works well for positive reinforcement; you might clarify whether the behavior (posting) increases because of the likes (positive reinforcement) or because checking for likes becomes compulsive due to variable ratio scheduling. Your misconception point is good but slightly buried — consider making it its own sentence.

Prompt 2: Good. Can you suggest one sentence I could add about variable ratio scheduling without adding much length?

AI response 2: Try: “Social media mimics a variable ratio schedule — likes arrive unpredictably, which produces the highest rates of checking behavior, just as slot machines produce persistent play.”

Optional Variations

  • Single-session version: Run both workflows back-to-back in one class period with 15 minutes per workflow. Reduce comparison to three questions. Recommended for introductory courses where awareness is the goal.
  • Class debrief version: Students share their logs anonymously (whiteboard or polling). Compare interaction counts and output lengths across the class. The aggregate makes the collective dimension visible without requiring specific environmental claims.
  • Faculty demonstration: Instructor completes both workflows live on screen — first unplanned, then planned with student input on the prompt. This is high-impact for resistant faculty audiences: it demonstrates that the instructor is learning alongside students.
  • Provided-output version: Use the transcripts above (Transcript A and Transcript B) for no-device classrooms or as a shared baseline for whole-class comparison. Students complete Parts 3 and 4 from the provided materials. All analytical and reflection work remains their own.
