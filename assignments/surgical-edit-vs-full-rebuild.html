Surgical Edit vs. Full Rebuild

Why Use This Assignment?

One of the most common things students do with AI and their own writing is paste it in and say “make this better.” The full rewrite that comes back is usually fluent and polished. It may also replace examples, flatten voice, alter meaning, or perform revision decisions the student was supposed to practice. Because fluent prose is often judged as more accurate and credible independent of content (Reber & Schwarz, 1999), students need a method for testing whether the polish actually improved the writing.

This assignment has students run both workflows on a paragraph they actually wrote: a full rebuild, then a surgical edit — three targeted, minimal improvements they apply by hand. The audit does not assume which workflow is superior. Students trace which of their own sentences survived, whether the named weakness was fixed, what was lost or improved, whether meaning changed, and what each workflow cost in generated output and corrective work.

For faculty worried that AI will write their students' papers, this is practical counter-programming: it teaches students to define and defend authorship rather than treating revision as an all-or-nothing delegation. It also makes resource proportionality concrete by asking whether the larger intervention generated enough additional value to justify its scope.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Execute and compare two AI revision workflows: full rewrite and targeted minimal edits.
  2. Audit an AI rewrite for both gains and losses — repaired weaknesses, preserved ideas, erased examples, flattened voice, or altered meaning — rather than judging it on polish alone.
  3. Explain how fluency influences quality judgments, and why a rewrite can feel better without necessarily being better.
  4. Formulate a personal rule for choosing edit scope that preserves authorship, learning, and resources.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

When you hand AI your writing and ask for a rewrite, you make a trade you may not have examined: AI performs more of the revision, while you retain less direct control over each change. That trade may or may not be worthwhile. A targeted-edit workflow keeps more decisions with you, but it may also leave weaknesses a full rewrite would catch. This assignment has you run both workflows on your own writing and decide from evidence which intervention was more useful. By the end, you will have a rule you wrote for yourself about when, if ever, to ask for a full rewrite.

Task

  1. Select your paragraph. Use a paragraph you wrote for this course (150 to 250 words) — a concept explanation, discussion post, or short response your instructor approves. It should be mostly adequate but honestly imperfect. Do not polish it first. The rough edges are the experiment.
  2. Stake your claim. Before any AI contact, underline the two or three sentences that best capture your own thinking — your signature sentences. Then write one sentence naming the paragraph's biggest weakness as you see it.
  3. Workflow A — Full Rebuild. Run exactly this prompt:
    Rewrite this paragraph to make it better: [your paragraph]
    Copy the output. Note its approximate word count. Do not request revisions.
  4. Workflow B — Surgical Edit. In a new chat, run exactly this prompt:
    Do not rewrite this paragraph. Identify the three highest-impact improvements — at the word or sentence level — and for each one, show the minimal edit that fixes it. Leave everything else unchanged: [your paragraph]
    Now apply the edits you agree with by hand to produce Version B. You may reject any edit — note which and why.
  5. Run the audit. Complete the comparison table for Workflow A vs. Workflow B:
    Audit questionWorkflow A (rebuild)Workflow B (surgical)
    Did your signature sentences survive? (verbatim / paraphrased / gone)
    Did your named weakness actually get fixed?
    What, if anything, got better? Use specific evidence.
    What, if anything, got worse or more generic? If nothing did, document what survived and why.
    Did the AI change your meaning anywhere, or introduce a factual claim you didn't make?
    Approximately how many words did AI generate?
    How many follow-up corrections would this version need before you'd submit it?
  6. Verify before adopting. If either workflow introduced or altered any factual claim, check it against your textbook or notes before it may appear in your final version. Record what you checked and the verdict.
  7. Choose and finish. Produce your final paragraph. It may be Version A, Version B, a hybrid, or your original with different edits — but every sentence in it must be one you can explain and claim. In one sentence, explain why you chose this version.
  8. Write your rule and reflect. In one sentence, state your personal rule: when is a full rewrite justified, and when is a surgical edit the right call? Then reflect (150 to 250 words), answering all three:
    • One workflow may have sounded better on first read. What does that feeling tell you — and what does it not tell you?
    • What did the rebuild preserve, improve, erase, or flatten that the audit made visible?
    • What thinking did you do in Workflow B that Workflow A would have done for you? Is that thinking you can afford to outsource in this course? In your career?

Guiding Questions

  • Whose examples are in the rebuilt paragraph — yours or ones AI substituted?
  • If you submitted Workflow A's output, what could you honestly claim to have written?
  • Why does the surgical edit require more of your judgment, not less, than the rebuild?
  • Did the larger intervention produce enough additional value to justify its scope?
  • Which workflow would you rather explain to your instructor — or an employer — afterward?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Steps 3 and 4 only — one full-rebuild prompt and one surgical-edit prompt, as written. No revision rounds on either.

Not allowed: Using AI to complete the audit table, choose or assemble the final version, write the personal rule, or write the reflection. Running additional rewrite rounds. Submitting either AI output unchanged as the final paragraph.

Required disclosure: I ran one full-rewrite prompt and one surgical-edit prompt on my own paragraph. I applied or adapted the following edits and rejected these: _____. My final version is my own; any AI-introduced factual change was verified against a course source.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools. Choose a paragraph free of personal disclosures.

Verification rule: Any factual claim the AI added or altered must be checked against course materials before it may appear in your final version.

Learning protection rule: The audit, the hand-applied edits, the final assembly, and the reflection are your work. The assignment exists to protect exactly the thinking an unexamined rewrite could replace.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • start from an honestly imperfect paragraph, with signature sentences and a named weakness staked out before any AI contact
  • complete every row of the audit with specifics — quoted phrases and named examples, not impressions
  • test whether each workflow produced gains or losses and support either conclusion with concrete evidence
  • catch and verify any meaning or factual changes either workflow introduced
  • end with a final version the student can fully claim, and a personal rule specific enough to follow

Student Submission Checklist

  • original paragraph with signature sentences underlined and weakness named (pre-AI)
  • Workflow A prompt and complete output
  • Workflow B prompt and complete output, with accepted/rejected edits noted
  • completed audit table, all seven rows, both columns
  • verification record (or “no factual changes introduced” statement)
  • final paragraph with one-sentence justification
  • personal rewrite-vs-edit rule
  • reflection answering all three questions
  • AI disclosure statement

Instructor Notes

Regression toward generic register — what to look for in the debrief. Full rewrites can pull student prose toward a generic register: hedged topic sentences, “furthermore/moreover” connectors, generalized examples, and a smooth, interchangeable voice. An illustrative example (construct your own from a real student paragraph, with permission):

Student original: “My roommate swears her lucky pencil got her through stats, which is really just an illusion of control with an eraser on it.”
Typical rebuild: “Many individuals attribute their successes to superstitious objects, which research suggests reflects the illusion of control.”

Nothing in the rebuild is factually wrong. Something distinctive is gone — the concrete case, the voice, and the joke that demonstrates understanding. This is one possible outcome, not a required finding. Some rebuilds will preserve the student's examples and repair the named weakness more effectively than the surgical edit. Reward accurate diagnosis rather than loyalty to the assignment's title.

Expect fluency to influence first impressions, and name why. Fluently written text is judged as more accurate and credible independent of content (Reber & Schwarz, 1999) — the same effect that makes AI feedback hard to evaluate in The Helpful Wrong Tutor. Signature-sentence survival, the named-weakness check, and explicit gain/loss rows convert that first impression into testable evidence.

Rebuilds sometimes change meaning silently. AI rewrites can sharpen a hedge into a stronger claim, swap a student's example for a different one, or add a factual assertion the student never made. This will not happen in every class set, which is why the assignment asks students to check rather than assume. If a student finds no meaning change, that is a legitimate result when supported with evidence.

The offloading frame. Offloading cognitive work to external tools is not inherently bad — it is a core, well-documented human strategy (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). The question this assignment teaches students to ask is which work to offload. A full rebuild transfers more revision decisions to AI; a surgical edit keeps more local decisions with the student. Neither fact alone determines which workflow produced the better paragraph.

Resource framing, stated carefully. The rebuild will usually generate more output than the surgical edit. The relevant question is whether that additional output produced enough improvement to justify the larger intervention and any correction burden. Frame this as proportionality — use the smallest intervention that does the job — not as carbon accounting. Do not attach specific environmental numbers to the comparison. This assignment pairs with The Efficiency Ethic as an ecological sequence: that assignment is about planning a whole task efficiently; this one is about choosing edit scope within a revision. See also docs/protocol-pack.md (Resource Rule, AI Use Triage).

Choosing paragraphs. Input quality affects the comparison. An already-polished paragraph may leave little for either workflow to improve; a paragraph the student does not value offers little authorship to protect. An honest early-semester draft the student has some investment in is the most informative input. Reusing a graded discussion post works well and requires zero new writing.

Relationship to nearby assignments: The Helpful Wrong Tutor teaches sorting AI feedback on an explanation the student keeps rewriting themselves; this assignment is about edit scope — what happens when AI touches the text directly. Run Helpful Wrong Tutor first if using both; students arrive already skeptical of fluent feedback.

Optional Variations

  • Counterbalanced order: Half the class runs the surgical edit first, half the rebuild first, then compare audits. Order effects on how students rate each version become a live metacognition discussion.
  • Pair audit: Students swap packets and audit each other's Workflow A output for preserved voice, erased voice, and changed meaning. Effects in someone else's writing can be easier to see than in your own.
  • No-device / provided-output version: The instructor supplies a sample student paragraph (with permission, or self-written) plus pre-generated outputs from both workflows; students complete the audit, final version, rule, and reflection. A legitimate pedagogical choice — including for instructors who limit AI use for ecological reasons — not merely an accessibility fallback.
  • Discipline-writing version: Upper-level students run both workflows on a methods section or literature review paragraph, where small wording changes may alter technical meaning. The audit becomes noticeably harder and more valuable.
  • Semester habit: After this assignment, permit AI revision help on future papers only with an edit-scope rationale and accepted/rejected change log attached. The assignment becomes the course's revision policy.
