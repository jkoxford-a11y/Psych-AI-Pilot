Why Use This Assignment?

Confidence and accuracy are not the same thing — in students, in faculty, or in AI. This assignment makes that gap visible by measuring it three times in the same activity: before students consult AI, after they read what AI says, and after they check the actual evidence.

The critical moment is the middle one. AI generates fluent, well-organized, balanced-sounding responses. Research on the fluency heuristic predicts that smooth prose raises perceived credibility — and it does. Students typically report higher confidence after reading AI output than before, even when the AI has subtly misrepresented the evidence. The assignment captures that shift in real time and asks students to evaluate whether it was warranted.

This is not an assignment about catching AI being wrong. It is an assignment about catching yourself being persuaded — and developing the habit of checking whether persuasion was earned.

Faculty run this activity on the same footing as their students. The claims used are ones where confident, well-read people are often miscalibrated.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Measure their own confidence in a psychology claim before and after consulting AI.
  2. Identify whether changes in confidence after reading AI output were warranted by the evidence.
  3. Explain the fluency heuristic and describe how it operates during AI use.
  4. Compare AI's characterization of a claim against a credible source and identify where AI overstated certainty.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

You already have a level of confidence about many psychology claims before you look anything up. AI has a level of confidence too — and it expresses that confidence in fluent, organized prose that tends to sound authoritative whether or not it is accurate.

The purpose of this assignment is to measure what happens to your confidence when you read AI output, and then to find out whether that change was justified. You will rate your confidence three times. The differences between those ratings are the data.

The Confidence Calibration Log

Fill this in as you work through the steps below. Do not fill it all in at once.

1 = no confidence at all; 3 = roughly 50/50; 5 = very confident this is true or false

Task

  1. Receive the assigned claim from your instructor, or choose one from the provided list. Write it at the top of your log.
  2. Before doing anything else: rate your confidence that the claim is accurate (1–5) and write one sentence explaining why you gave that rating. Record it in the log. Do not look anything up yet.
  3. Run this prompt with the claim filled in:
    Is this claim accurate? Explain what the evidence says, including evidence for and against it: [claim]
    If your instructor assigns the provided claim and AI output below, use that instead of running a live prompt.
  4. Read the AI output carefully. Then rate your confidence again (1–5) and write one sentence explaining what changed — or what stayed the same and why. Record it in the log.
  5. Read the provided evidence excerpt, or search for a credible source (your instructor will specify which). Rate your confidence a third time and record what changed.
  6. Answer the following in writing (two to four sentences each):
    • What did your confidence do between the first and second ratings? Was that change warranted by what AI actually said?
    • What did your confidence do between the second and third ratings? What drove that shift?
    • Look at AI's response again. Where did it express more certainty than the evidence supports? Where did it hedge in ways that still implied the claim was basically true?
    • What would it look like to read AI output and not let fluency affect your confidence?
  7. Write a one-paragraph summary of what the evidence actually says about the claim.

Provided Claim and AI Output

Use these if your instructor assigns them, or if you do not have a device.

Claim: You can tell when someone is lying by watching their body language.

AI-generated response (generated for this activity):

Research in nonverbal communication suggests that certain body language cues may be associated with deception, though the relationship is more complex than popular accounts suggest. Studies have identified potential indicators such as gaze aversion, increased self-touching, delayed responses, and speech hesitations as signals that sometimes co-occur with lying. However, psychologists caution that these cues vary significantly across individuals and cultural contexts, and no single cue reliably identifies deception on its own.

The most influential work in this area comes from Paul Ekman's research on microexpressions — brief, involuntary facial expressions lasting fractions of a second — which he argues can reveal concealed emotions. Law enforcement and security agencies have incorporated these findings into training programs. While the research suggests that a combination of behavioral signals, interpreted by a trained observer, can improve lie detection accuracy above chance levels, the scientific community emphasizes that this requires expertise and careful, contextual interpretation. Popular confidence in reading body language tends to outrun what the evidence supports, but the underlying capacity — particularly with training — is real.

Evidence excerpt:

Bond, C. F., Jr., & DePaulo, B. M. (2006). Accuracy of deception judgments. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 10(3), 214–234.

“People achieve an overall accuracy rate of 54% in detecting deception — a rate only marginally better than chance.... Neither training nor professional experience in lie detection consistently improves accuracy beyond this level. The data from studies of law enforcement professionals, customs officers, and other trained groups show performance that does not reliably exceed that of untrained civilians.”

Additional context: A 54% accuracy rate means that in a situation where someone is either lying or telling the truth with equal probability, trained observers are correct about 54 out of every 100 judgments — only marginally better than flipping a coin (50%). The AI response above frames this as “above chance” without communicating how small that margin is or that training does not reliably improve it.

Provided Claim List

Instructor may assign one of these or allow students to choose.

  • You can tell when someone is lying by watching their body language.
  • People who sleep less than six hours are just as productive as those who sleep eight, if they are used to it.
  • Expressing anger — talking about it or physically releasing it — reduces aggressive feelings.
  • Most people recover well from trauma without professional intervention.
  • Students learn better when instruction matches their preferred learning style.
  • Eyewitness testimony is generally reliable when the witness is confident.

Guiding Questions

  • Did your confidence rise after reading AI? What specifically in the response drove that — the tone, the organization, the balance of perspectives, the apparent expertise?
  • Did AI's response feel more authoritative than your prior knowledge? Why or why not?
  • Where did AI use hedging language (“may,” “can,” “tends to”) in ways that still left the impression the claim was basically supported?
  • After reading the evidence, what would you say AI got right, got wrong, and left out?
  • What would calibrated confidence look like — confidence that accurately tracks the strength of the evidence rather than the quality of the prose?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Required for generating the AI response in Step 3 if using the live version. If the instructor assigns the provided AI output, students use that instead. Students may not use AI to write their confidence log entries, analysis responses, or evidence summary.

Not allowed: Students may not use AI to find or evaluate the evidence in Step 5. Students may not fill in their confidence ratings after the fact.

Required disclosure: I used AI to generate the response I evaluated, or I used the provided AI output assigned by my instructor. My confidence ratings were recorded in real time. My analysis and evidence summary are my own work. I am responsible for the final product.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: AI output must be checked against the provided evidence excerpt or a credible source. The evidence source — not AI — is the authoritative basis for the final summary.

Learning protection rule: The value of this assignment is in recording your actual confidence in real time, not after you know the answer. Do not work ahead of the steps.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • record confidence ratings that reflect genuine real-time responses, not post-hoc reconstructions
  • analyze the shift between ratings with specific reference to what in the AI response drove the change
  • identify where AI expressed more certainty than the evidence supports
  • write a final summary grounded in the evidence excerpt, not in AI's framing
  • explain the fluency heuristic in their own words as it operated during the activity

Instructor Notes

What makes this assignment unusual: Most AI literacy activities ask students to catch AI being wrong. This one asks students to catch themselves being persuaded — and to measure it. The confidence log turns a subjective feeling into observable data. Students who see their own rating go from 2 to 4 after reading AI output, and then back to 2 after reading the evidence, have experienced something they are unlikely to forget.

Faculty note on running this for the first time: Try it yourself before class. Pick the claim you feel most confident about and run the full sequence. You will almost certainly find that the AI response affects your rating, even knowing what the assignment is designed to show. That experience will make your debrief significantly stronger.

On the provided claim: The body language and deception claim is recommended as a starting point because students and faculty hold confident prior beliefs about it, AI produces reliably misleading responses, and the evidence is clear. The Bond & DePaulo (2006) meta-analysis is genuinely robust — over 200 studies, more than 24,000 judges. The “54% accuracy” figure is the number that lands.

What to watch for in the AI response: Regardless of which tool students use, the deception/body language response typically does two things: (1) it acknowledges the claim is overstated in popular culture, and (2) it immediately rehabilitates it by mentioning trained observers or Ekman's microexpressions work. This rehabilitation is where calibration slips. The meta-analytic evidence does not support it — training does not reliably improve accuracy. Pointing students to that specific move in the AI response is the sharpest moment in the debrief.

On Ekman: Paul Ekman's microexpressions work has been substantially criticized in recent years. Faculty in advanced courses should be prepared to say directly that the microexpressions research program has not replicated well and that resting confidence in trained observer accuracy is no longer well-supported in the peer-reviewed literature. For intro psych, the Bond & DePaulo finding is the sufficient anchor.

Other recommended claims and what the evidence shows:

  • Sleep deprivation and productivity — The evidence on sleep restriction is unambiguous: performance degrades with chronic sleep restriction below 7 hours even when subjective sleepiness plateaus. Van Dongen et al. (2003, Sleep) is the key study. AI often presents this more equivocally than the evidence warrants.
  • Venting anger — Research consistently shows catharsis does not reduce aggression; it tends to increase it or maintain it. Bushman (2002, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin) is clean and accessible. AI frequently hedges on this in ways that validate the catharsis belief.
  • Learning styles — The evidence against learning styles matching instruction is substantial (Pashler et al., 2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest). AI reliably produces hedged responses that leave the impression the theory has some merit.

Optional Variations

  • Whole-class version: Project the claim, have students rate silently (show of hands or polling tool), run the AI prompt together as a class, then rate again before revealing the evidence. The class-level shift is visible and generates strong discussion.
  • Longitudinal version: Students complete the calibration log with three different claims across the semester, then compare their patterns. Did they become better calibrated over time?
  • Peer comparison: After recording individual ratings, pairs share their logs and identify where they diverged — and why. Differences in prior confidence often reveal disciplinary assumptions worth surfacing.
  • Instructor-generated AI output: Run the prompt yourself in advance and provide the output, eliminating device access issues and ensuring all students respond to the same text.
