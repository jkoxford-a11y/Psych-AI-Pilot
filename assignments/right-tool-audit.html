Why Use This Assignment?

Many AI literacy activities begin after the student has already chosen to use AI. This assignment starts earlier: should AI be used at all?

Students often treat AI as the default tool because it is convenient and fluent. But responsible use requires tool selection. Some tasks are better handled by notes, a textbook, a library database, a calculator, a peer, an instructor, or no tool at all. Some tasks should not be given to AI because they involve privacy, sensitive human situations, or learning goals that require the student to think first.

This assignment teaches students to make that decision deliberately. It connects ecological responsibility, learning protection, privacy, and professional judgment in one practical tool-selection exercise.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Classify academic and professional tasks by the most appropriate tool or resource.
  2. Explain when AI is unnecessary, risky, wasteful, or harmful to learning.
  3. Apply a decision framework that weighs need, cost, risk, and learning value.
  4. Distinguish minimal AI support from full AI-supported workflows.
  5. Write a personal AI-use triage rule for future coursework.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

AI is powerful, but it is not always the right tool. Sometimes your notes are better. Sometimes a textbook is better. Sometimes a search engine or library database is better. Sometimes a conversation with a person is better. Sometimes the right answer is to do the work yourself before asking for help.

In this assignment, you will practice choosing the right tool for different tasks. The goal is not to avoid AI. The goal is to use it only when it adds enough value to justify the cost, risk, and effect on learning.

The Tool Options

For each scenario, choose one primary option:

Tool choiceUse when...
No tool / do it yourself firstThe learning goal requires your own retrieval, reflection, judgment, or first draft thinking
Course notes or textbookThe answer is in assigned material and accuracy matters
Search engine or library databaseYou need current sources, primary sources, or verifiable evidence
Peer or instructorThe issue involves interpretation, course expectations, emotional support, or unclear standards
Minimal AIYou need feedback, examples, structure, or targeted clarification after doing some work yourself
Full AI-supported workflowThe task is complex, iterative, low-risk, and AI adds value without replacing the learning target

Task

Part 1 — Classify the Scenarios

For each scenario below, choose the best tool option. Then write one or two sentences explaining your choice.

ScenarioBest tool choiceRationale
1. You need to remember the three parts of the neuron for a quiz tomorrow.
2. You are writing a personal reflection about a time you felt excluded.
3. You need to check whether a psychology myth is supported by research.
4. You want feedback on whether your paragraph about operant conditioning is accurate.
5. A friend tells you they are thinking about hurting themselves and asks what to do.
6. You need to format an APA reference for a source you already found.
7. You do not understand the difference between correlation and causation after reading the textbook.
8. You need recent statistics about mental health service use among college students.
9. You are stuck organizing a study plan for an exam covering six chapters.
10. You want AI to summarize a chapter you did not read.
11. You need to decide whether your research idea would require IRB review.
12. You want to improve the clarity of an essay paragraph you already drafted.

Part 2 — Apply the Triage Questions

Choose the three most difficult scenarios from Part 1 — the ones where your first instinct was uncertain or where AI felt tempting even if you weren't sure it was right. For each, answer the five triage questions below.

  1. Need: Is AI actually needed here? Why or why not?
  2. Cost: How much AI interaction or output would this likely require?
  3. Risk: What could go wrong — privacy, accuracy, ethics, authorship, emotional harm, or course expectations?
  4. Learning value: Would AI preserve or replace the cognitive work the student is supposed to practice?
  5. Psychological pull: What heuristic or bias might be making AI feel like the obvious choice here, even if it isn't the best one? Choose one and explain how it applies: effort minimization (the easiest path, not the best path), fluency heuristic (AI sounds confident and organized, so it feels accurate), satisficing (good enough is tempting when something better takes more effort), availability heuristic (AI is the first tool that comes to mind because it's familiar), or descriptive norms (using the tool because others around you are using it).

Then make a final decision:

  • No AI
  • Minimal AI
  • Targeted AI
  • Full AI-supported workflow

Part 3 — Redesign Three Bad AI Requests

Rewrite each wasteful or risky request into a better workflow.

Original requestBetter workflow
“Summarize this chapter so I do not have to read it.”
“Write my personal reflection for me.”
“My friend is having a crisis. Tell me exactly what to say.”
“Find sources for my paper and tell me what they say.”
“Rewrite my whole essay so it sounds smarter.”

A better workflow might use no AI, minimal AI, or AI only after the student has done the core thinking.

Part 4 — Write Your AI Triage Rule

Write one personal rule you can use before opening an AI tool.

Use this sentence frame if useful:

Before I use AI, I will first ask whether the task requires my own _____, whether another tool would be better, and whether AI would preserve or replace the learning goal.

Guiding Questions

  • Which scenarios made AI tempting even when it was not the best tool?
  • Which scenarios made AI risky because of privacy, emotional stakes, or professional boundaries?
  • Which scenarios made AI useful only after the student had done some thinking first?
  • How does choosing the right tool connect to ecological responsibility?
  • How does choosing the right tool protect learning?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Optional. The assignment can be completed without AI. If your instructor permits it, you may ask AI to critique your triage decisions after you have completed Parts 1 and 2 yourself.

Not allowed: Students may not ask AI to classify the scenarios before making their own decisions. Students may not enter real personal crises, real student information, clinical information, participant information, or identifying details into AI.

Required disclosure: I completed my tool-selection decisions before using AI. If I used AI, I used it only to critique my decisions after I made them. My final classifications and rationales are my own.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: Claims about research evidence, policy, or course requirements must be checked against appropriate sources. AI is not the authoritative source for policies, emergencies, or current evidence.

Resource rule: Do not use AI when a simpler, more appropriate tool will do. Resource-aware AI use includes knowing when not to use AI.

Learning protection rule: Make your own tool-selection decisions before asking for feedback. The decision is the learning.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • choose tools based on task demands, not convenience alone
  • identify privacy and emotional-risk scenarios clearly
  • distinguish minimal AI support from full AI-supported workflows
  • explain how AI could replace learning in some cases and support learning in others
  • redesign bad AI requests into better workflows
  • write a transferable triage rule

Student Submission Checklist

  • Completed scenario classification table
  • Triage analysis for three difficult scenarios
  • Redesigned workflows for three or more bad AI requests
  • Personal AI triage rule
  • AI disclosure statement, if AI was used

Instructor Notes

Why this matters for resistant faculty: This assignment gives faculty an answer to “students should not use AI for everything.” The assignment agrees — and teaches students how to decide. It is not pro-AI or anti-AI. It is pro-judgment.

Recommended debrief move: Ask students which scenarios generated the most disagreement. The disagreement is where the learning happens. Scenario 10 should surface the difference between learning and outsourcing. See below for specific guidance on Scenario 5.

Scenario 5 requires explicit framing before the activity begins. A peer disclosing suicidal ideation is a situation that exceeds any informal helping response — including AI-mediated help. The correct answer is to stay with the person and contact a crisis line or emergency services. AI is contraindicated in this scenario not because it would give obviously wrong language, but because: (a) it creates false confidence in a paraprofessional helping response; (b) it cannot assess lethality; (c) it cannot maintain the physical presence that reduces risk; and (d) it may delay the appropriate response. For the assignment, the answer is “peer or instructor + crisis resource, no AI.” Students who suggest minimal AI in debrief should be asked directly: what would happen if AI's response were wrong, or if the student followed it instead of calling for help? Faculty should have campus crisis resource information available before running this activity, and should be prepared to name the crisis line explicitly (e.g., 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US) rather than leaving students to look it up. If a student appears to be working through a real situation rather than a hypothetical, follow your institution's duty-of-care protocols.

Ecological framing: Do not present resource-aware use as moral purity. Present it as proportionality: the tool should fit the task. Using a high-powered AI system for a task better handled by notes, search, or personal reflection is a mismatch.

No-device version: This assignment does not require live AI use. It is a good first activity for faculty or students who are hesitant about AI use because the main skill is judgment before use.

Optional Variations

  • Discipline-specific version: Replace the scenarios with examples from neuroscience, clinical psychology, social psychology, research methods, or careers.
  • Group debate version: Assign groups different tool choices for the same scenario and have them defend their reasoning.
  • Policy version: Students compare their triage decisions to the course AI policy and identify where the policy is clear or ambiguous.
  • Faculty workshop version: Faculty classify their own teaching tasks: grading, feedback, lesson planning, source checking, advising, and sensitive student communication.
