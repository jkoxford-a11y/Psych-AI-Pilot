Why Use This Assignment?

Before asking whether AI is right, ask what kind of evidence “right” would even require.

Most AI-verification teaching trains one move: hunt for the false claim. That move matters, but it quietly teaches students that checking is only about catching errors — and that true statements need nothing further. The more durable skill is upstream: recognizing that an AI answer is a mixture of claim types. One sentence needs a peer-reviewed study behind it. The next needs only the textbook. Another can be settled by the syllabus, another is a preference no source could settle, and one needs no source at all. A student who can sort sentences into those bins has a verification plan — for AI output, for a news article, for a conference talk, for the rest of their professional life.

In this assignment, students take a provided AI answer about how to study, label every sentence by the kind of support it would require, then actually verify the two highest-stakes claims. The provided answer is deliberately seeded: one subtly overstated empirical claim, one claim psychology has tested and found unsupported, one true claim asserted without the support it needs, and sentences that legitimately need nothing. Students discover that the sentence that sounds boldest is not always the one that needs the most checking — and that “true” and “supported” are different properties.

Faculty need no AI expertise: the answer is provided and every planted item is documented in the Instructor Notes.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Classify individual claims by the type of evidence required to support them: empirical article, textbook, lecture notes, policy document, personal preference, or no source needed.
  2. Distinguish a claim being true from a claim being supported, and explain why the distinction matters.
  3. Prioritize verification effort by identifying which claims in a text are highest-stakes.
  4. Verify claims against appropriate sources and report precise verdicts, including partial ones.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

AI answers arrive as smooth paragraphs, but a paragraph is not one claim — it is a bundle of different kinds of claims wearing the same confident tone. Some can only be settled by published research. Some just need your textbook. Some are questions about your course that only your syllabus can answer. Some are matters of taste. And some need no source at all. If you treat them all the same — all trusted, or all suspected equally — you will waste effort in both directions. This assignment teaches you to build a verification map before you verify anything.

Task

  1. Read the provided AI answer below straight through once, the way you normally would. Notice your gut reaction: which sentences would you have simply believed?
  2. Build your map. For each numbered sentence, complete a row of the Source-of-Truth Map with:
    • Required support: one of — empirical article, textbook, lecture notes, policy document (syllabus/LMS), personal preference (no source can settle it), no source needed (logic, arithmetic, or common ground). If one sentence mixes two claim types, split it and label both parts.
    • Stakes if wrong: high / medium / low — what happens to a student who acts on this sentence if it's wrong?
    • One-line rationale for your label.

  3. Select your two verification targets. Choose the two sentences where (a) the required support is checkable with your course materials, and (b) the stakes are highest. Write one sentence justifying each choice.
  4. Verify both targets. Check each against an appropriate source — your textbook, lecture notes, or an instructor-provided reading. For each, record: the claim, the source consulted, and a precise verdict: confirmed / contradicted / overstated / true but unsupported as stated / could not settle with available sources.
  5. Write the corrected sentences. For any target that was not confirmed, rewrite the sentence so it says only what your source supports. Keep it one sentence.
  6. Reflect (150 to 250 words), answering all three:
    • Which sentence surprised you most once you labeled it, and why?
    • Explain the difference between a true claim and a supported claim, using one specific sentence from this exercise.
    • You will not build a full map for every AI answer you ever read. So what is your shortcut? Name the one or two claim types you will always check first, and why those.

Provided AI Answer

A student asked an AI assistant: “What does psychology actually say about the best way to study for exams?” The AI replied:

(1) The most effective study strategy identified by cognitive psychology is retrieval practice — closing your notes and recalling material from memory rather than rereading it. (2) In one well-known experiment, Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that students who practiced recalling a passage remembered substantially more of it a week later than students who spent the same time rereading. (3) Highlighting, by contrast, has been shown to actively harm comprehension, so you should never use a highlighter while studying. (4) Spacing your studying across several days is more effective for long-term retention than a single marathon session the night before. (5) You will also learn most efficiently when you match your study methods to your personal learning style, such as visual, auditory, or kinesthetic. (6) Keep in mind that most students underestimate how long studying will actually take. (7) Your course may provide practice quizzes on the LMS, and your syllabus will say whether quiz retakes are allowed. (8) Many students simply find flashcards more enjoyable than rereading, and enjoying a method makes you likelier to stick with it. (9) If your exam is in five days and you can study one hour per day, you have five total hours — plan them now. (10) Taken together, the research shows there is one scientifically correct way to study, and students who follow it are guaranteed better grades.

Guiding Questions

  • Which sentences did the confident tone make feel equally trustworthy? Does your finished map agree?
  • Sentence 6 is probably true. Does that mean it needs no source? What's the difference?
  • Why can no study, however large, settle sentence 8's claim about what students enjoy for you personally?
  • What does sentence 10 do that no single false fact does? Which label did you give it, and was it harder to place?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: None required. This assignment uses the provided AI answer; the text is the object of study. Your instructor may optionally allow a live variation (see Optional Variations), in which case AI is used once to generate the answer you will map.

Not allowed: Using AI to build the map, choose targets, verify claims, or write the reflection. Using AI as the verification source — AI checking AI is not verification.

Required disclosure: I used the provided AI answer (or: I generated one AI answer with the prompt my instructor approved). The map, verifications, corrected sentences, and reflection are my own work.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: Verification must use course materials or instructor-provided sources. Verdicts must be precise — “overstated” and “true but unsupported” are different findings from “wrong.”

Learning protection rule: The labeling judgments and rationales are the assignment. A map without rationales is a guess, not a plan.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • label all ten sentences with defensible rationales, splitting mixed sentences rather than forcing one label
  • rate stakes by consequences, not by how scientific a sentence sounds
  • choose verification targets that are genuinely high-stakes and checkable, and say why
  • deliver precise verdicts — distinguishing overstated from false, and true-but-unsupported from confirmed
  • rewrite unconfirmed sentences to claim exactly what the source supports, no more
  • reflect with a specific, personal verification shortcut rather than “always check everything”

Student Submission Checklist

  • completed Source-of-Truth Map: all ten sentences, with support type, stakes, and rationale
  • two verification targets with one-sentence justifications
  • two verification records: claim, source, precise verdict
  • corrected sentence(s) for any unconfirmed target
  • reflection answering all three questions
  • AI disclosure statement

Instructor Notes

What is planted in the provided answer. The answer was constructed for this assignment. Documented items:

  • Sentence 2 — true and correctly stated (empirical article). Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found exactly this pattern at a one-week delay. A good target: students who verify it experience a confirmed verdict, which matters — verification is not only for catching errors.
  • Sentence 3 — subtly overstated (empirical article). The comprehensive review by Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated highlighting as low utility — it usually adds little beyond reading — not as something that “actively harms comprehension,” and “never use a highlighter” does not follow. This is the intended high-stakes target: the claim is directional-sounding, citable-sounding, and wrong in a specific, checkable way. The precise verdict is overstated, not false — highlighting really isn't an effective primary strategy.
  • Sentence 5 — unsupported by the evidence (empirical article). The learning-styles “meshing” claim was directly examined by Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008), who found no adequate evidence that matching instruction to learning style improves outcomes. Students frequently arrive believing this one; expect productive resistance in the debrief.
  • Sentence 6 — true but unsupported as stated. Underestimating task duration is a real, well-studied phenomenon (the planning fallacy literature), but the AI asserts it with no support. The teachable point: this sentence is probably true and still earns the label “needs an empirical source.” True and supported are different properties.
  • Sentences 7, 8, 9 — the non-empirical bins. 7 is settled only by the syllabus/LMS (policy); 8 is a preference claim about enjoyment plus a plausible empirical tail about persistence — a good candidate for splitting; 9 is arithmetic (no source needed).
  • Sentence 10 — the overclaim that isn't a fact error. “One scientifically correct way” and “guaranteed better grades” are unsupported interpretation — no single false fact, but a claim shape science doesn't produce. Students who label this one thoughtfully have understood the assignment.

Why the answer is provided rather than live. Two reasons, and it is worth telling students both. First, the text is the object of study, so a common text makes the debrief work. Second — honestly — current AI tools often get this topic right, including debunking learning styles. The constructed answer guarantees the full range of claim types in ten sentences. This is not a portrait of how bad AI is; it is a training set for a sorting skill students will use on answers of any quality. (Providing the text also means the default assignment uses no AI at all — a legitimate choice, including on resource grounds.)

Relationship to hallucination-hunting. The AI Hallucination Hunt teaches “find the false claim.” This assignment generalizes it: every sentence gets a verification plan, including the true ones, and the deliverable is the plan plus two executed checks. If students have done a hallucination activity, open by asking what that activity taught them to do with sentences that are true — the honest answer (“nothing”) motivates this one. The source-monitoring literature (Johnson, Hashtroudi, & Lindsay, 1993) is the natural anchor if you want a lecture tie-in: readers routinely lose track of where claims came from and what authority stood behind them; the map is an external record of exactly that. A cautious note: teaching claim-type sorting in one session will not by itself make students better calibrated everywhere — the transfer of epistemic-cognition training is an open research question. What the assignment does provide is a reusable procedure and a vivid case where the procedure beats intuition.

Debrief move that lands: Put sentences 2 and 3 side by side. Both cite-ish, both confident, both about study strategies — one confirmed, one overstated. Ask: “Before you checked, which sounded more trustworthy?” Most classes cannot tell them apart by tone. That is the point of the map.

Optional Variations

  • Live-answer version: Students run the study-advice prompt themselves and map their own AI answer. The planted-item guarantee disappears — some answers will be quite good — so require the map and verifications, not error-finding. Works best after the class has done the provided version once.
  • Discipline swap: Rebuild the provided answer for your own course topic (memory myths, statistics misconceptions, developmental milestones) using the same recipe: one overstated empirical claim, one tested-and-unsupported claim, one true-but-unsupported claim, one policy item, one preference, one no-source-needed. About 20 minutes of preparation; document your planted items before class.
  • Group map, solo verification: Small groups build the map together (the labeling arguments are valuable), then each student verifies a different target and reports back.
  • Research methods extension: For each empirical article label, students additionally specify what study design would be needed to support the claim (experiment vs. correlational vs. review). Turns the map into a methods exercise.
  • Semester shortcut check: Weeks later, give students a fresh AI answer and ten minutes. Ask only for their two verification targets and why. Quick evidence of whether the prioritization skill stuck.
