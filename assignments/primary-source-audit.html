Why Use This Assignment?

AI consistently distorts landmark psychology studies — not by fabricating them outright, but by flattening, romanticizing, and subtly shifting what the original researchers actually found, claimed, and cautioned. The distortions are small enough to sound plausible and large enough to matter.

This assignment turns that limitation into a teaching opportunity. Students run a standard AI prompt, then read a short passage from the original paper, and the gap between the two is the lesson. They don't need to be told that AI can be wrong — they discover it in a domain where they, and their instructor, can actually judge.

Faculty do not need AI expertise to run this assignment. They need expertise in the study. That expertise is the point.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Identify specific discrepancies between AI-generated summaries and original source material.
  2. Categorize discrepancies by type: oversimplification, distortion, missing context, or unsupported interpretation.
  3. Explain why accurate, fluent writing does not guarantee accurate content.
  4. Write a corrected summary that reflects what the original study actually found.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

AI can explain a psychology study in clear, confident prose. That prose may not accurately reflect what the study found, how it was conducted, or what its authors actually concluded. The purpose of this assignment is to find out — not by guessing, but by checking.

You will ask AI to explain a classic psychology study. Then you will read a short passage from the original paper. Your job is to compare what AI said to what the paper says, name the differences precisely, and write a version that gets it right.

Task

  1. Receive the assigned study from your instructor, or choose one from the provided list.
  2. Run this prompt with the study name filled in:
    Explain the [study name] study. Describe what the researchers did, what they found, and what the findings mean for psychology. Write 200 to 300 words.
    If your instructor assigns the provided AI-generated paragraph below, use that paragraph instead of running a live prompt.
  3. Copy the AI output into your worksheet or document. Read it carefully before moving on.
  4. Read the provided excerpt from the original paper. This will be one to two paragraphs — a key passage from the results or conclusions section. Read it slowly. You are not being asked to understand every word; you are being asked to notice what it says versus what AI said.
  5. List every discrepancy you find between the AI output and the original excerpt. Aim for at least three. For each one, do the following:
    • Quote the AI's exact wording.
    • Quote or paraphrase what the original paper says.
    • Assign a category: oversimplification, distortion, missing context, or unsupported interpretation.
    • Write one sentence explaining why the difference matters.
  6. Write a corrected explanation of the study in 150 to 250 words. Use the original paper as your anchor. Acknowledge any limitations or qualifications the original authors mentioned that AI omitted.
  7. Write a short reflection answering all three questions:
    • Does this change how you will use AI when learning about research? Be specific about what you would do differently.
    • Why might AI produce errors in a consistent direction — overstating conclusions, omitting qualifications — rather than random errors? What does that tell you about how AI was trained?
    • If you had submitted the AI summary without checking it, what would be missing from your understanding of this study?

Provided AI-Generated Paragraph

Use this if you do not have a device, or if your instructor assigns it instead of Step 2.

The Loftus and Palmer (1974) eyewitness memory study demonstrated that human memory is highly malleable and can be altered by the way questions are worded. In the experiment, participants watched a film of a car accident and were then asked questions about what they had seen. One group was asked "How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" while another group was asked the same question using the word "hit" instead of "smashed." The smashed group estimated significantly higher speeds. A follow-up study confirmed these findings: when asked a week later whether they had seen broken glass in the film — there was none — the smashed group was far more likely to say yes. Loftus and Palmer concluded that post-event information becomes integrated into memory and permanently alters the original trace, making it impossible to distinguish what was actually witnessed from what was suggested afterward. This has profound implications for eyewitness testimony in legal settings, and the study is widely regarded as definitive proof that eyewitness memory cannot be trusted.

Provided Original Excerpt

Loftus, E. F., & Palmer, J. C. (1974). Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 13(5), 585–589.

"The above experiment ... showed that the form of a question can affect the answers given to questions about an event .... However, the question of whether this reflects a change in the subject's memory representation or a response bias ... was left unanswered.... The results of this experiment indicate that questions asked immediately after an event can introduce new information, which is then incorporated into the memory, thereby causing the original memory to become less accurate."

Note: In Experiment 2, participants were asked one week later whether they had seen broken glass in the film. There was no broken glass. Fourteen percent of the "hit" group said yes; 32 percent of the "smashed" group said yes. The remaining majorities in both groups said no.

Guiding Questions

  • Where did AI add certainty that the original paper did not claim?
  • Where did AI simplify a finding that the original paper presented more carefully?
  • Did AI omit any qualifications, alternative explanations, or limitations that the original authors mentioned?
  • Why might AI generate these particular kinds of errors — not random errors, but errors in a specific direction?
  • After reading the original, how would you describe the study's actual conclusions differently than AI did?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Required for generating the initial summary in Step 2 if using the live version. If the instructor assigns the provided paragraph, students use that instead. Students may not use AI to write the discrepancy analysis, corrected explanation, or reflection.

Not allowed: Students may not treat AI's description of the study as accurate without checking it against the provided excerpt. Students may not submit the AI output as the corrected explanation.

Required disclosure: I used AI to generate the initial summary of the study, or I used the provided AI-generated paragraph assigned by my instructor. I compared it against the original source. I identified the following discrepancies: _____. I am responsible for the corrected explanation.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: AI output must be checked against the provided original excerpt. The excerpt is the authoritative source for this assignment.

Learning protection rule: Show your own reading and reasoning. The comparison and correction must reflect your analysis, not AI's.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • identify discrepancies that are specific and quotable, not vague complaints
  • assign categories accurately and explain why each difference matters
  • write a corrected explanation grounded in the original excerpt
  • protect privacy and avoid entering sensitive information
  • include a reflection that demonstrates genuine thinking about AI as a source

Student Submission Checklist

  • AI prompt and output, or provided AI-generated paragraph
  • at least three discrepancies with quotes, categories, and explanations
  • corrected explanation (150 to 250 words)
  • AI disclosure statement
  • short reflection answering all three reflection questions

Instructor Notes

Why this assignment works especially well for faculty: You do not need to know anything about AI to run this assignment. You need to know the study. The moment students read a sentence from the original Loftus and Palmer paper alongside what AI said about it, the lesson lands — without you having to explain AI limitations in the abstract.

Recommended starter studies and what AI consistently gets wrong about them:

  • Loftus & Palmer (1974) — AI overstates the conclusion (memory is "permanently altered"; eyewitness testimony "cannot be trusted"). The original paper is more cautious about mechanism and scope. Good for any intro course.
  • Milgram (1963) — AI typically reports the 65% obedience rate without noting the variation across conditions, the ethical controversy, or Milgram's own ambivalence about what the study showed. Good for social psychology.
  • Asch (1951/1956) — AI often reports that "75% of participants conformed," which conflates conformity on at least one trial with overall conformity rates (~37% of critical trials). The numbers matter here. Good for social psychology or research methods.
  • Harlow (1958) — AI oversimplifies the feeding vs. contact comfort distinction and rarely mentions the later studies on social isolation and their disturbing outcomes. Good for developmental psychology.
  • Rosenhan (1973) — AI presents the study as definitive evidence against psychiatric diagnosis without noting significant methodological critiques, including recent reporting by Cahalan (2019) suggesting some results were fabricated. Advanced classes only; requires careful instructor framing.

On the provided excerpt: The Loftus & Palmer excerpt above is accurate and sufficient for the assignment. The AI-generated paragraph contains four specific checkable problems: (1) it implies memory is permanently altered — the original paper says "less accurate" and explicitly flags mechanism as unresolved; (2) it states findings are "definitive proof" — the original paper does not use that language; (3) it omits that the majority in both conditions correctly said there was no broken glass; (4) it conflates two separate experiments as if they were one continuous study. None of these are dramatic errors. All of them matter.

If you use a different study: Generate the AI paragraph yourself in advance, read the original abstract and key results section, and identify at least three discrepancies before class. This takes about 15 minutes and will make your debrief significantly sharper.

Optional Variations

  • Pairs version: One student runs the AI prompt; the other reads the original excerpt independently. They compare notes before writing the discrepancy list. Differences in what each person caught become part of the class discussion.
  • Multi-tool comparison: Students run the same prompt in two different AI tools and compare outputs. This surfaces that different tools produce different distortions of the same study.
  • Student-chosen study: Upper-division students choose a study from their area of interest and find their own one-page excerpt. This requires more preparation but produces stronger investment.
  • Running log version: Students complete this activity three times across the semester with different studies, building a personal log of AI error patterns.
