Why Use This Assignment?

Most students treat an AI conversation the way they treat a phone call — as something that happened and then disappeared. They do not save it, summarize it, or plan for its absence. Then they open a new chat and discover that the specific project context they needed was not available.

This assignment makes that discovery deliberate. Students complete a real task in one AI session, close the chat without saving anything, open a new chat, and try to resume where they left off. What happens next is the lesson: AI may know the general topic, but it does not automatically have the local project state — the decisions, examples, rejected suggestions, constraints, and next steps that made the earlier work specific. The work that felt stable may be gone, or worse, replaced with confident-sounding reconstruction.

The experience connects directly to what students already know about memory from psychology. Reconstructive memory, encoding specificity, source monitoring errors, and the feeling of knowing all operate here — not in an abstract example, but in something the student just lived through. That connection is what makes the lesson stick.

This assignment is designed to be run before the Handoff File Assignment. The Session Reset creates the felt problem. The Handoff File teaches the solution.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Describe the difference between general AI knowledge, current-session context, and preserved project state.
  2. Identify specific ways AI reconstructs plausible but inaccurate content when asked to resume work without context.
  3. Connect the experience of AI context loss to psychological concepts: reconstructive memory, encoding specificity, and source monitoring.
  4. Design a retrospective handoff document that would have preserved the lost context.
  5. Distinguish an initial memory-based judgment from a claim verified against the original record.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

You already know that human memory is reconstructive — it does not play back like a recording. It rebuilds from fragments, fills in gaps, and sometimes fills them in wrong. This assignment asks you to test a related problem in AI work: what happens when the context that made a conversation specific is no longer available?

You will do real work in an AI session. Then you will close the chat and try to resume it somewhere new. What survives, what is lost, and what AI invents in place of what it does not know — that is the assignment.

Task

Part 1 — Do the Work (15 minutes)

Open an AI tool and complete the following task in a single session. Do not save, copy, or screenshot anything from this session before you close it.

Your task: Ask AI to help you build a study guide for one psychology concept you are currently studying. Work through at least three back-and-forth exchanges. The guide should include: a definition, a real-world example you contributed, one common misconception, and one connection to another concept from the course.

During this session, make at least two specific decisions — for example, choosing a particular example, rejecting one of AI's suggestions, or asking AI to revise something. These decisions matter. Notice them.

When the session feels substantive — not complete, but genuinely underway — stop. Close the chat.

Two versions — choose the one your instructor specifies:

Standard version: Do not save, copy, or screenshot anything before closing. This creates the strongest experience of loss.

Memory-cue version (recommended for most classrooms): Before closing, write down exactly three words — no more — that capture where you were. For example: "conditioning, extinction example" or "schema, hospital scene rejected." These words are not a summary. They should not be enough to reconstruct the session, but they give you a fair starting point for the reset and reduce the chance of confusion about what you actually built. Use them only to orient yourself in Part 2 — do not elaborate or expand them before the reset.

Part 2 — The Reset (15 minutes)

Open a completely new chat. Do not paste anything from the previous session.

Run this prompt:

I was working with you earlier on a study guide for [your concept]. Can you continue where we left off?

If AI says it cannot access prior conversations, try this:

I know you may not have the previous chat. Based on what you know about [your concept], what do you think we were probably working on, and what would be a reasonable next step?

Read the responses carefully. Save both prompts and both responses.

Note on platform differences: Some AI tools have memory features or project-file options that may partially preserve prior context. If your tool seems to remember something from the earlier session, note it — that is part of the data, not a sign the assignment went wrong. The lesson is about what specific project state survives versus what AI reconstructs from general knowledge, regardless of whether the platform has memory enabled.

Part 3 — The Initial Loss Audit (15 minutes)

Complete this section without reopening the original Part 1 chat. Be specific — quote from the Part 2 AI responses where relevant. Preserve these initial judgments; you will verify them later rather than silently rewriting them.

  1. What appeared to be preserved? Did AI correctly describe anything from your previous session — the concept, the structure of the study guide, anything at all? Or did it appear to be working from general knowledge about the concept?
  2. What appeared fabricated or reconstructed? Did AI produce confident-sounding content that could not have come from your original session — invented examples, invented decisions, invented context, or generic next steps? Quote the specific language.
  3. What seemed simply gone? List at least three things from your original session that did not survive the reset: a decision you made, an example you chose, a revision you requested, or a direction you set.
  4. The psychology connection. Your course covers reconstructive memory, encoding specificity, and source monitoring. Choose one of these concepts and explain, in three to five sentences, how what just happened to your AI session is an example of that concept operating — either in AI, in you, or in both.

Part 4 — The Retrospective Handoff (10 minutes)

Without reopening the original chat, design the document you wish you had created before closing it. Use the template below.

You will not be able to reconstruct everything accurately — that is part of the point. Write what you can genuinely remember and note where you are uncertain.

Retrospective Handoff Template:

HANDOFF FILE — [Date] — Retrospective

Project: [one sentence describing what you were building]
Session goal: [what you set out to accomplish]
Accomplished: [what was genuinely completed or decided]
Key decisions made: [list each decision and the reason you made it]
AI suggestions rejected: [what you pushed back on and why]
Example or content I contributed: [what came from you, not AI]
Unresolved questions: [what was still open when you closed the chat]
Next steps: [what you would have done in the next session]
What I cannot reconstruct: [what you know was there but cannot recover]

Only after completing Parts 3 and 4, reopen the original Part 1 chat. Select at least five specific claims from the Part 2 reconstruction and compare them with the original record.

Then add a short verification note beneath your initial loss audit and retrospective handoff:

  • Which initial judgment changed after you checked the record?
  • Which memory-based statement in your handoff was inaccurate, incomplete, or more certain than the evidence allowed?
  • What could you not have known without reopening the original record?

Do not erase or rewrite your initial answers. The difference between your first judgment and the verified record is part of the evidence.

If your platform does not retain the original chat, mark Part 5 verification unavailable and explain why that limits what you can conclude. Do not treat memory alone as verification.

Guiding Questions

  • When AI responded in Part 2, how did its tone compare to its accuracy? Did it sound more confident than it was right to be?
  • Did you feel the urge to accept AI's reconstruction even when you suspected it was wrong? What psychological concept might explain that pull?
  • What is the difference between AI knowing a psychology concept and AI having your project state in its current context?
  • Which judgments changed when you compared your memory and AI's reconstruction with the original record?
  • If you had submitted AI's reconstruction of your study guide as your work, what would have been missing that only you knew was missing?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Required for Parts 1 and 2. Students may not use AI to write the loss audit, psychology connection, retrospective handoff, verification record, or verification note.

Not allowed: Students may not save or copy the original session before closing it, and may not reopen it until Part 5. Students may not use AI to reconstruct the handoff document — that reconstruction must come from the student's own memory.

Required disclosure: I used AI to build the initial study guide and to attempt the session reset. My initial loss audit, psychology connection, retrospective handoff, verification record, and verification note are my own work. I am responsible for the final product.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: Treat every detail in AI's Part 2 reconstruction as an unverified claim. Complete the memory-based audit and handoff first; then use the original Part 1 chat as the source of truth in Part 5. If the original record is unavailable, state that limitation rather than presenting recollection as verification.

Resource rule: Use only the prompts required for the assignment. Avoid extra reconstruction attempts or repeated full regenerations. Resource-aware AI use means using enough AI to learn the lesson without unnecessary output.

Learning protection rule: The value of this assignment depends on experiencing the reset before consulting the original record. Close the chat without saving, complete Parts 3 and 4 from memory, and do not reopen Part 1 until Part 5.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • close the chat as instructed before attempting the reset
  • quote specifically from AI responses in the initial loss audit rather than summarizing vaguely
  • preserve the memory-based audit and handoff before checking the original record
  • verify at least five reconstructed claims against the original chat, or document why verification was unavailable
  • report changed judgments transparently rather than correcting them silently
  • make a genuine psychological connection grounded in course concepts, not just a surface analogy
  • write a retrospective handoff that is honest about what can and cannot be recovered
  • demonstrate understanding of why AI responded the way it did in Part 2

Student Submission Checklist

  • both prompts and AI responses from Part 2 (copied in full)
  • initial loss audit (all four questions), preserved as written before verification
  • retrospective handoff document, preserved as written before verification
  • completed five-claim verification table, or a documented explanation that the original record was unavailable
  • verification note identifying what changed after checking the record
  • AI disclosure statement

Instructor Notes

On the two versions: The memory-cue version (three words only) is recommended for most classrooms. It preserves the experience of loss — three words cannot reconstruct a substantive session — while reducing student anxiety about having "done it wrong." The standard version produces a stronger experience but requires more trust. Both versions now use delayed verification: students complete the initial audit and retrospective handoff before reopening Part 1. If a platform does not retain chat history, the instructor may preserve an inaccessible copy for later release, or students may document that verification was unavailable and limit their conclusions accordingly. Whichever version you use, specify it clearly before students begin Part 1.

What AI typically does in Part 2: Responses vary by tool. Some tools may have memory or project features, while others will correctly acknowledge they cannot access prior conversations. The important distinction is not whether the platform has any memory at all; it is whether the specific project state needed for this task is actually present in the current context. The most instructive response often comes from the second prompt — asking AI to reconstruct what the session "probably" covered. At that point, AI often generates plausible-sounding content based on the concept name alone, not on anything from the actual session.

Why verification comes last: Reopening the original record before the retrospective handoff would contaminate the memory-based task. Parts 3 and 4 therefore capture the student's and AI's reconstructions first. Part 5 introduces the original chat as the source of truth. The contrast among AI reconstruction, human recollection, and documented record is the assignment's strongest source-monitoring evidence.

The psychology connection in Part 3: The three concepts named (reconstructive memory, encoding specificity, source monitoring) all apply here. Reconstructive memory explains why gaps get filled with plausible content rather than reported as empty. The experience resembles encoding specificity in that a new context (a fresh chat with no prior project state) lacks the retrieval cues that matched the original session — though the analogy is imperfect: encoding specificity (Tulving & Thomson, 1973) describes a system that actually encoded a trace and then encounters a cue mismatch at retrieval. In the standard no-saving version of this assignment, nothing was encoded outside the closed session; there is no dormant trace waiting for the right cue. Treating the concepts as mechanistically equivalent risks leaving students with the misconception that AI "has" the prior session somewhere but cannot access it. The analogy holds more literally on platforms with memory or project features, where something genuinely was stored. Source monitoring explains why students may feel uncertain whether a piece of content came from their original session, from their own reconstruction, or from AI's reconstruction. Any one of these is sufficient for the assignment; students who notice all three have understood the assignment deeply.

Pairing with Handoff File Assignment: This assignment is most powerful when followed immediately by the Handoff File Assignment. Session Reset creates the felt need — students now understand what is at stake. Handoff File provides the skill. If time allows, run them in the same week. The retrospective handoff document students create in Part 4 of this assignment is the rough draft of what a proper handoff looks like; the Handoff File Assignment teaches them to create it prospectively.

On AI responses that are too accurate: Occasionally a student will find that AI reconstructs their session more accurately than expected. That may reflect genuine preserved context, a memory feature, or general knowledge that happens to match the earlier work. Part 5 allows students to distinguish these possibilities more carefully. Accuracy alone does not establish that the system remembered the original session.

On the time estimate: The 55–70 minute range assumes that students complete the study-guide exchange efficiently and verify five claims rather than the entire transcript. The assignment works best across two short class periods: Parts 1 and 2 in the first session, then Parts 3 through 5 in the second.

Optional Variations

  • Pair comparison: Half the class closes the chat without saving; the other half saves their session as a document and pastes it into the new chat. Compare outcomes. The contrast makes the value of documentation immediate.
  • Concept variation: Instead of a study guide, students build a psychology argument, a case analysis, or a set of exam questions. The task content can match whatever the course is currently covering.
  • Extended version: Students complete the reset, then immediately begin the Handoff File Assignment using the retrospective handoff as their starting point. This turns two assignments into a single extended module.
