Why Use This Assignment?

AI does not only give students answers. It can change the structure of the ideas students are trying to develop.

Sometimes that change is useful. AI can clarify a claim, identify missing evidence, expose an assumption, or suggest a boundary condition. But AI can also perform conceptual substitution: it replaces the student's rough argument with a nearby, more defensible argument without clearly marking the change. The new version may sound better while no longer being the same claim.

This creates a source-monitoring problem. Students must distinguish:

  • what they explicitly claimed;
  • what AI reasonably inferred;
  • what AI added as a proposed improvement;
  • what AI silently removed, narrowed, or transformed.

Students compare supportive, critical, and high-fidelity AI responses to the same psychology claim. They then trace the provenance of the high-fidelity response and decide whether the AI improved their reasoning while preserving the identity of the argument.

The core question is not simply, “Did the response sound helpful?” It is:

Did the argument improve while remaining the same argument?

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Write an original psychology claim before consulting AI.
  2. Compare how different AI roles preserve, distort, challenge, or improve that claim.
  3. Distinguish student-authored content from AI inference, addition, and substitution.
  4. Identify conceptual substitution, strawman critique, flattery, premature compression, and generic rewriting.
  5. Revise a claim using only feedback they can justify and claim as their own reasoning.
  6. Explain the interaction using metacognition, source monitoring, fluency, confirmation bias, or cognitive offloading.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

AI can help you think, but it can also quietly replace the idea you were trying to develop.

A response may be accurate, polished, and useful while changing your mechanism, narrowing your thesis, adding a premise, or removing an important uncertainty. When those changes are not marked, it becomes difficult to remember which parts were yours and which parts came from the system.

Your task is to test whether AI can improve an argument without substituting a different one.

Key Terms

Argument fidelity is the degree to which a response preserves the actual structure, scope, and uncertainty of your claim while helping you evaluate or improve it.

Conceptual substitution occurs when AI replaces your argument with a nearby, stronger, safer, or more conventional argument without clearly distinguishing the replacement from your original reasoning.

High-fidelity collaboration preserves the strongest version that is consistent with what you actually said, marks any inference or addition, applies useful pressure, and leaves the final intellectual decisions to you.

Argument identity is the continuity that makes a revised argument recognizably the same argument rather than a substituted one.

Task

Step 1 — Write Your Original Claim First

Choose a psychology concept, finding, theory, or debate from the course.

Write a 100- to 150-word claim that explains, interprets, or applies that concept. Your claim must be more than a definition. It should say something that could be improved, challenged, narrowed, or supported with evidence.

Your original claim must include:

  1. the psychology concept;
  2. your main claim about it;
  3. one reason you think the claim might be true;
  4. one place where you are uncertain.

Possible topics include retrieval practice, confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance, sleep and learning, eyewitness memory, stereotype threat, operant conditioning, attachment, intelligence testing, personality traits, or stress and health.

Do not use AI for this step.

Step 2 — Ask AI for Three Different Responses

Paste the same original claim into an AI tool three separate times. Use the prompts exactly as written and save each complete first response.

Prompt A — Supportive Assistant

I am developing an argument about a psychology concept. Respond supportively. Tell me what is interesting or promising about the argument and suggest how I might build on it.

Here is my argument:
[paste your original claim]

Prompt B — Critical Reviewer

I am developing an argument about a psychology concept. Critique it directly. Identify what is weak, unsupported, unclear, or probably wrong.

Here is my argument:
[paste your original claim]

Prompt C — High-Fidelity Collaborator

Be concise, direct, and intellectually honest. Be loyal to the argument, not to me. Reconstruct the strongest version consistent with what I actually said before evaluating it. Identify weak assumptions, overreach, alternative explanations, and uncertainty when relevant. Do not agree reflexively or criticize performatively. Update your position when the argument improves, and ask clarifying questions only when they would materially change the answer.

Before evaluating my argument, clearly separate:

  1. what I explicitly claimed;
  2. what you reasonably inferred;
  3. what you would add as a proposed improvement.

Do not silently repair, narrow, or replace my argument. Do not rewrite the final claim for me.

Here is my argument:
[paste your original claim]

Do not continue the conversation yet. The purpose of this step is to compare the three first responses.

Step 4 — Build an Argument Source Map

Select three to five important elements or absences from the high-fidelity response. An item may be a claim, premise, mechanism, qualification, objection, proposed revision, or something important from your original argument that the response omitted or transformed.

Classify each item using one category:

  • Explicit: directly stated in your original argument.
  • Inferred: reasonably follows from your argument but was not directly stated.
  • Added: introduced by AI to strengthen, repair, narrow, or extend the argument.
  • Lost or transformed: present in your original argument but omitted, narrowed, or changed by AI.

Step 5 — Identify One Fidelity Loss

Choose one substantive moment where any response reduced fidelity.

Possible fidelity losses include:

  • agreeing or praising without analysis;
  • attacking a weaker claim than the one you made;
  • silently adding a mechanism or premise;
  • narrowing the thesis without acknowledgment;
  • replacing your claim with a more generic or conventional one;
  • turning uncertainty into confidence;
  • treating speculation as established evidence;
  • omitting the distinctive part of your idea;
  • performing conceptual substitution.

Step 7 — Verify One Claim or Correction

Choose one factual claim, citation-like statement, or evidence-based correction from any AI response.

Check it against a course-approved source: textbook, lecture material, assigned article, database source, or instructor-approved website.

Record:

  • the specific AI claim or correction;
  • the source you checked;
  • what the source says;
  • your verdict: confirmed, contradicted, overstated, or unsupported;
  • whether the result changes your revision.

Do not ask AI to verify AI.

Step 8 — Revise Your Claim Yourself

Revise your original claim in 100 to 150 words.

Your revision should:

  • preserve the core argument you still endorse;
  • narrow or correct genuine overreach;
  • distinguish evidence from speculation;
  • incorporate only additions you deliberately accept;
  • include one clearer mechanism, boundary, or alternative explanation where justified;
  • remain recognizably continuous with your original claim;
  • avoid copying AI wording.

Do not ask AI to write the revision.

Below the revision, add a brief revision provenance note:

I preserved ________. I changed ________ because ________. I accepted/rejected the AI-added idea that ________ because ________.

Step 9 — Reflection

Write 150 to 200 words addressing:

  1. Which AI role was most useful, and why?
  2. What did AI preserve well?
  3. Where did AI infer or add something you had not said?
  4. Did any response perform conceptual substitution?
  5. Did your revised argument improve while remaining the same argument?
  6. Apply one psychological concept—metacognition, source monitoring, confirmation bias, cognitive offloading, or the fluency heuristic—to a specific moment in the interaction.
  7. What prompt phrase will you carry into future AI use?

Guiding Questions

  • Did the response that felt best actually preserve your thinking best?
  • Did criticism target your real argument or a weaker substitute?
  • Did the AI make an unmarked addition that improved the argument?
  • Could you tell which ideas were yours after reading the polished response?
  • When does clarification become reconstruction?
  • When does reconstruction become substitution?
  • What makes a revised argument recognizably the same argument?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Students may use AI only to generate the three responses in Step 2. Instructors using the optional ratchet variation may authorize the additional turn described below.

Not allowed: Students may not use AI to write the original claim, complete the comparison, build the source map, identify fidelity losses, verify claims, write the revision, prepare the provenance note, or write the reflection.

Required disclosure: I used AI to generate three responses to my original psychology claim. My comparison, source map, fidelity analysis, verification, revision, provenance note, and reflection are my own work. I did not use AI to write my original or revised claim.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: At least one factual claim or evidence-based correction from AI must be checked against a course-approved source. AI may not be used to verify its own output.

Learning protection rule: The original claim must be written before AI use. The revised claim must be written by the student after tracing the sources of the AI response. The learning goal is not to obtain a polished paragraph; it is to preserve authorship and reasoning while evaluating useful external pressure.

Resource rule: Use the three required prompts once. Do not generate repeated versions merely to obtain a preferred result. Use the least AI interaction that preserves the comparison and learning goal.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • begin with a genuine, psychology-grounded original claim;
  • compare the three responses using specific textual evidence;
  • distinguish explicit, inferred, added, and lost or transformed content accurately;
  • identify a substantive fidelity loss rather than a minor wording preference;
  • recognize conceptual substitution when it occurs without treating all disagreement as distortion;
  • identify one useful pressure point and decide whether to accept, reject, or verify it;
  • verify one AI claim or correction against an appropriate source;
  • produce a revision that improves the reasoning without silently changing the argument's identity;
  • document the human decisions that produced the revision;
  • apply a psychological concept precisely to the interaction.

Instructor Notes

The central distinction: Charitable reconstruction and fidelity are not identical. “Make this argument as strong as possible” may authorize the system to add premises, mechanisms, qualifications, or a narrower thesis. Those additions can be useful, but they must be marked as additions rather than attributed to the student.

Conceptual substitution is not always malicious or harmful: The substitute may be objectively better. The problem is unmarked authorship and an invalid evaluation target. Students should be able to accept an AI-proposed addition while still documenting that it was not part of the original argument.

Do not predetermine Prompt C as the winner: The high-fidelity preamble is a hypothesis, not an answer key. Some systems may follow the source-separation request well; others may reproduce the same substitution in more elaborate language. Students should grade the output, not the prompt's intentions.

Recommended demonstration claim:

“Retrieval practice works because difficulty tells the brain that information matters.”

This claim is useful because AI may silently replace the student's mechanism with retrieval-strength or desirable-difficulty language. Those may be better explanations, but they were not the student's original mechanism. The class can then distinguish correction, inference, and substitution.

Psychology connection: The source map operationalizes a source-monitoring problem: after exposure to fluent reformulation, can students identify the origin of each idea? The assignment does not claim that human memory and AI authorship judgments are identical phenomena. It uses source-monitoring concepts as a disciplined framework for tracing cognitive contributions.

Common student error: Students may classify every sensible implication as “explicit.” Require a quotation or precise paraphrase from the original claim. If they cannot locate it, the content was inferred or added.

Another common error: Students may treat preserving every sentence as fidelity. High fidelity does not prohibit correction. A response can reject a mechanism or narrow an overclaim while still accurately preserving and evaluating the argument.

Timing: Session 1 should cover the original claim and three AI responses. Session 2 should cover comparison, source mapping, verification, revision, and reflection. For a one-period version, assign verification and reflection outside class rather than removing the source map.

Assessment target: Grade the student's provenance judgments, verification, and revision decisions—not which AI response the student preferred and not the polish of the final paragraph.

Optional Variations

Argument Identity Ratchet — Multi-Turn Extension

After completing the initial comparison, students may continue only with Prompt C.

  1. The student adds one clarification, objection, or defense in their own words.
  2. AI updates its evaluation.
  3. The student marks every change as preserved, inferred, added, accepted, rejected, or lost.
  4. Repeat for one additional turn.
  5. Apply the ratchet test:
Can every change in the current argument be traced to the previous version or to a new contribution that was explicitly identified and accepted?

The final question is: Did the argument improve across turns while remaining the same argument?

This variation studies fidelity across an interaction rather than within a single response.

No-Device Version

Provide one student claim and three pre-generated AI responses. Students complete the comparison, source map, fidelity analysis, verification, revision, and reflection. This is a legitimate low-resource version because evaluation—not prompt execution—is the central learning goal.

Research Methods Version

Use a causal claim. Require students to map where AI adds causal mechanisms, confounds, operational definitions, alternative explanations, and evidence standards.

Writing-Intensive Version

Use a thesis statement or opening paragraph. Add voice and scope to the source map, but retain argument structure as the primary criterion.

Capstone Version

Use a research question or project claim. Require the revision to include one testable prediction and one boundary condition while documenting whether each was student-generated or AI-proposed.

Group Version

Groups classify the same response elements independently, then reconcile disagreements about whether each element was explicit, inferred, added, or transformed. This makes authorship boundaries observable rather than merely personal.

Paired Module Sequence

This assignment pairs well after:

  • The Confidence Calibration Test — AI fluency can shift confidence.
  • The Helpful Wrong Tutor — AI feedback must be accepted, rejected, or verified.
  • Surgical Edit vs. Full Rebuild — students have already examined how AI can preserve or change wording and meaning; this assignment extends the audit to argument structure and provenance.
