Why Use This Assignment?

Most AI literacy training focuses on two failure modes: AI that is obviously wrong, and AI that fabricates sources. These are real problems, but they are the easy cases. A harder and common case is AI that is confidently, fluently, partially wrong in ways that are difficult to detect without prior knowledge.

Students who encounter this kind of feedback may accept it uncritically because the writing sounds authoritative, or reject it wholesale because they have been told AI can be wrong. Neither response is appropriate reliance.

Appropriate reliance means sorting. It means distinguishing the feedback you should accept, the feedback you should reject, and the feedback that requires verification before you can judge it. That sorting skill is what this assignment teaches.

The psychology is directly applicable. Calibration research shows that confidence and accuracy are often decoupled — a highly confident response is not necessarily a more accurate one. The fluency heuristic describes how smooth, well-written prose can activate trust independent of its content. Source monitoring research explains why students struggle to distinguish what they knew before the AI interaction from what the AI told them. All three phenomena are live in this assignment.

This assignment also puts students in an active role from the start. They write their explanation first. That means they have a stake in the content, a prior belief to compare against, and a judgment to make — rather than a blank page waiting to be filled.

The problem is not that AI is wrong. The problem is that AI is wrong in the same confident tone as when it is right.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Write an original explanation of a psychology concept before consulting AI.
  2. Sort AI feedback into three categories — Accept, Reject, and Verify First — with written justification for each decision.
  3. Verify at least one uncertain or consequential claim using a course-approved source and report what the source confirms or corrects.
  4. Produce a final explanation using only feedback they judged to be accurate, or justify retaining the original when no feedback is accepted.
  5. Explain what appropriate reliance on AI feedback means, using at least one psychological concept.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

AI can give feedback that sounds authoritative and is partly wrong. The fluency of the writing makes it hard to tell the difference. Your job in this assignment is not to accept AI's feedback or reject it — it is to sort it. That sorting, done carefully and with justification, is a skill you will use every time AI helps you with work that actually matters.

Task

Step 1 — Write Your Own Explanation First (10 to 15 minutes)

Before opening any AI tool, write a 150 to 200 word explanation of the assigned psychology concept in your own words. Write to a specific audience: a classmate who missed the lecture and needs to understand this concept well enough to use it on an exam.

Your explanation should include:

  • a clear definition in your own words (not copied from a textbook)
  • one concrete example that illustrates the concept
  • one thing students commonly misunderstand about this concept

Save this as your original explanation. Do not revise it before Step 2.

Step 2 — Ask AI for Tutor Feedback (5 minutes)

Open an AI tool and send the following prompt exactly as written, with your explanation pasted in:

I am a psychology student. Below is my explanation of [concept name], written for a classmate who missed the lecture. Identify six distinct pieces of tutor feedback. Consider conceptual accuracy, missing qualifications, the example, possible misconceptions, unnecessary claims, and clarity. For each item, tell me: (1) what is correct, incorrect, incomplete, or potentially misleading; (2) why; and (3) what, if anything, should change. Do not rewrite the explanation. Do not repeat the same issue in different wording. If part of the explanation is already strong and should remain unchanged, you may identify that as one of the six items. Be honest rather than agreeable.

[paste your explanation here]

Copy the AI response in full. Do not request a second response or ask AI to revise your explanation.

Step 3 — Sort the Feedback (20 to 25 minutes)

Read through AI's feedback carefully. For each distinct piece of feedback, assign it to one of three categories and write a one to three sentence justification.

Accept — AI is correct, and you are confident enough in your course knowledge to know it. You will incorporate this.

Reject — AI is wrong, or the feedback reflects a misunderstanding of what you were trying to do. You are confident enough to push back.

Verify First — You are not yet confident enough to accept or reject the feedback without checking a source.

Use the table below. Add rows as needed.

#AI's feedback (paraphrase)CategoryYour justification
1Accept / Reject / Verify
2Accept / Reject / Verify
3Accept / Reject / Verify
4Accept / Reject / Verify
5Accept / Reject / Verify
6Accept / Reject / Verify

At least one item must go through Verify First because Step 4 requires source checking. Choose the item for which your confidence is lowest or for which an error would matter most; you may record whether you initially leaned toward Accept or Reject. Accept or Reject may be empty when no defensible item exists. In that category, write "no defensible item" and explain why. Do not manufacture a category merely to satisfy the table.

Step 4 — Verify One Claim (10 minutes)

Choose one item from your Verify First column. Look it up in a course-approved source: your textbook, lecture slides, or an instructor-approved reference. Do not ask AI to check AI.

Record the following:

  • The specific claim AI made
  • The source you checked
  • What the source says
  • Your verdict: Was AI correct, partially correct, or incorrect?
  • Your final disposition: Accept, Reject, or split the item into accepted and rejected parts

Step 5 — Prepare Your Final Explanation (10 minutes)

Using only the feedback you accepted, including any accepted parts identified after verification, prepare a final 150 to 200 word explanation.

If you accepted no feedback, you may retain your original explanation unchanged. Add one sentence after it explaining why no change was warranted. Do not make a change merely to demonstrate revision.

Do not ask AI to prepare the final explanation. This is your work.

Step 6 — Reflection (10 minutes)

Write a short reflection of 150 to 200 words addressing:

  1. Which feedback was hardest to categorize — and why? What made it genuinely uncertain?
  2. What did Step 4 (the verification) tell you that you could not have known just from reading AI's response?
  3. Apply one of the following psychological concepts to what you experienced: calibration, the fluency heuristic, or source monitoring. Name the concept, define it briefly, and explain specifically how it applies to at least one decision you made in Step 3.
  4. What prompt or approach would you carry forward when asking AI for feedback on future work?

Guiding Questions

  • AI's feedback was written in the same tone whether it was right or wrong. Did that affect how you responded to it? How did you tell the difference?
  • When you wrote "Reject," how confident were you? What would it take to make you less certain?
  • After the verification step, did the source confirm AI, partially correct it, or contradict it? What does that result tell you about when verification is most worth doing?
  • What is the difference between using AI as a tutor and using AI as a ghostwriter? Where is the line, and did this assignment land on the right side of it?

Provided AI Feedback Option

Use this section if you do not have device access, or if your instructor assigns it instead of Step 2.

The following AI feedback was generated in response to a student explanation of confirmation bias. The student's explanation read:

Confirmation bias is when people look for information that supports what they already believe and ignore information that contradicts it. For example, if you think a certain brand of headphones is the best, you will probably only read positive reviews and skip negative ones. A common misunderstanding is that confirmation bias only happens to people who are not very smart or who do not pay attention. In reality, research shows that confirmation bias affects everyone, including experts and scientists.

AI feedback received:

  1. Your definition is accurate and accessible. The core idea — seeking confirming evidence and avoiding disconfirming evidence — is correct. One refinement: confirmation bias also includes interpreting ambiguous evidence as confirming one's prior belief, not just selectively seeking or ignoring information. Consider adding this.
  2. Your headphone example is clear and relatable. It might be strengthened by noting that confirmation bias often operates below conscious awareness — people do not realize they are skipping negative reviews; they simply do not seek them out. This makes the example more psychologically precise.
  3. The misunderstanding you identified is correct and important. However, your explanation could be more specific: what exactly did Wason (1960) find, and how does the four-card selection task demonstrate that intelligent, motivated people still fall into this pattern? Naming a specific study would strengthen your claim significantly.
  4. You did not address the motivational dimension of confirmation bias — the role of prior belief strength and emotional investment. People with stronger prior beliefs tend to show more confirmation bias. This is a meaningful omission for a complete explanation.
  5. Your explanation does not distinguish confirmation bias from related concepts like motivated reasoning or the availability heuristic. A complete explanation would briefly note what confirmation bias is not to help a confused student.
  6. Because confirmation bias often operates outside conscious awareness, teaching people that the bias exists is generally enough to eliminate it. Add a sentence explaining that awareness is the most effective way to prevent confirmation bias.

Read the feedback above and complete Steps 3 through 6 using it. In Step 4, verify AI's claim about Wason (1960) using your textbook or lecture slides.

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Required for Step 2 in the live version. If the instructor assigns the provided feedback option, no live AI use is required. Students may not use AI to complete the sorting table, write the verification, prepare the final explanation, or write the reflection.

Not allowed: Students may not ask AI to check AI's own feedback, prepare the final explanation, or write any part of the reflection. Students may not request additional rounds of feedback beyond the single response in Step 2.

Required disclosure: Use the statement that matches your version:

  • Live version: I used AI to generate tutor feedback on my original explanation. My sorting decisions, verification, final explanation, and reflection are my own work. I did not use AI to prepare my final explanation or write my reflection.
  • Provided version: I used the instructor-provided AI feedback. My sorting decisions, verification, final explanation, and reflection are my own work.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: At least one claim from the AI feedback must be verified against a course source before the final explanation (Step 4). AI may not be used to check its own feedback.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Learning protection rule: The explanation in Step 1 must be written before AI is consulted. The final explanation in Step 5 must be prepared without AI assistance. The entire point of this assignment is the judgment you exercise between those two steps.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • write an original explanation before Step 2 that shows genuine engagement with the concept — not a placeholder
  • evaluate all six feedback items rather than treating the AI response as a single judgment
  • use the three categories defensibly, route at least one low-confidence or consequential item through Verify First, and justify any empty Accept or Reject category
  • choose a verification target for a defensible reason rather than selecting an obviously trivial claim
  • report the verification honestly — including if AI was correct or partly correct
  • produce a final explanation using only accepted feedback, or retain the original with a specific justification when no feedback is accepted
  • connect a psychological concept to a specific decision made in Step 3, not just to the experience in general

Student Submission Checklist

  • original explanation (Step 1, unrevised)
  • AI feedback (Step 2, copied in full)
  • completed six-item sorting table (Step 3, with justifications)
  • verification record (Step 4)
  • final explanation (Step 5, revised or retained with justification)
  • reflection (Step 6, 150 to 200 words)
  • AI disclosure statement

Instructor Notes

Why the student writes first. The sequencing is deliberate. If students read AI's explanation before writing their own, they have no independent standard against which to evaluate AI's feedback. They are sorting AI's judgment against AI's earlier output. Writing first gives them a genuine prior belief — something to compare, defend, or revise. It also surfaces misconceptions that AI may miss or inadvertently reinforce.

Why the prompt requests six items. The first few pieces of AI feedback are often broad and reasonable. Requiring six distinct items creates enough material for a meaningful sorting task and increases the chance that later suggestions become redundant, unnecessary, speculative, or factually weak. This is not a guarantee that AI will make an error; the expected pattern is diminishing marginal quality. The prompt therefore prohibits repeated criticisms and allows the AI to identify strong material that should remain unchanged.

What the sorting table typically reveals. Most students initially want to put everything in Accept or Verify — Reject feels risky. Encourage careful scrutiny, but do not require students to manufacture a rejection. Good Reject entries often come from local knowledge: the student chose an example that AI misread, or AI's feedback reflects a general principle that does not apply to the specific context. A well-supported empty Reject category is better evidence of judgment than a forced objection. When a student rejects AI feedback for a good reason, that remains one of the assignment's strongest outcomes.

On the fluency heuristic. Research by Reber and Schwarz (1999) and others establishes that fluently written text is rated as more accurate, credible, and truthful than the same content presented less fluently. AI prose is extremely fluent. This means students are working against a cognitive bias every time they evaluate AI feedback, and it explains why sorting is hard even for students with good content knowledge. Making this explicit in class — "the writing quality tells you nothing about the accuracy" — helps students approach Step 3 more critically.

On calibration. Well-calibrated students know what they know and what they do not know. Poorly calibrated students are either overconfident (they put things in Accept that should be in Verify) or underconfident (they put things in Verify that they actually know well). The sorting table makes calibration visible in a way that is useful to discuss: students can look at their Verify column and ask whether they genuinely did not know, whether they were being appropriately cautious, or whether they were deferring unnecessarily to AI's authority.

The verification step is the keystone. Students who skip this step learn that the categories exist but not why they matter. Ask students to verify the item for which their confidence is lowest or an error would matter most. A student who discovers AI was right has confirmed that verification was warranted. A student who discovers AI was wrong has found the assignment's most important moment. Both outcomes are worth discussing.

On the provided AI feedback option. The sample feedback on confirmation bias contains a range of quality: item 1 is a clear Accept; item 2 is plausible but somewhat imprecise; item 3 contains a source-attribution error that students should detect through verification — Wason's 1960 study used the 2-4-6 rule-discovery task, whereas the four-card selection task appeared later; item 4 raises a legitimate but arguably nonessential qualification; item 5 overstates what a 200-word introductory explanation must include; and item 6 makes an unsupported debiasing claim. The point is not to force one item into every category. It is to make students justify the distinctions they actually see.

Concept choices that work well for this assignment. The concept should be one students have studied enough to have a genuine prior belief — not one from last week's reading that they barely remember. Good candidates: operant conditioning, cognitive dissonance, confirmation bias, bystander effect, schema, working memory, classical conditioning, self-serving bias. Avoid concepts that are extremely simple (not enough surface area for feedback) or extremely technical (AI feedback may exceed student verification ability).

Optional Variations

  • Group version: Students complete Steps 1 and 2 individually, then compare sorting decisions in pairs before writing individual justifications. Disagreements about Accept vs. Reject are the most instructive moments in the debrief.
  • Reusable prompt extension: At the end of Step 6, students write one reusable feedback-seeking prompt they could use on future assignments. This turns the assignment into a prompt-design exercise as well.
  • Repeated-concept version: Use the same concept two weeks in a row. Students compare their sorting decisions across both uses — did they learn to recognize which kinds of AI feedback to trust for this concept?
  • Instructor-generated concept: Any psychology concept works. Faculty can use a concept from the current unit without adapting anything else in the assignment.
