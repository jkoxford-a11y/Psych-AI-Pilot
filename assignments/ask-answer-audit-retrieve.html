Why Use This Assignment?

Answer first. AI second. Memory last — because the goal is what's left when the chat closes.

The most common way students use AI is also the worst way to learn from it: ask the question, read the fluent answer, feel understanding, move on. The feeling is real; the learning usually is not. Decades of research say the durable move is the opposite one — pull the answer out of your own memory first, even imperfectly, and let feedback refine what you generated. Practice testing is one of the most robust and well-replicated effects in learning research (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Dunlosky et al., 2013).

This assignment rebuilds the student-AI interaction around that finding. Students answer from memory before AI is allowed into the room, use AI only as a critic — never as an answer source — verify one of its claims, and then prove what stuck by retrieving again days later with everything closed. The structure makes the learning-protective workflow the path of least resistance, and the delayed retrieval makes the payoff visible to the student rather than asserted by the instructor.

Faculty do not need AI expertise to run it. You need one good exam-style question per student — something you already have.

Student Learning Outcomes

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  1. Apply retrieval practice by producing an answer from memory before consulting any source.
  2. Construct a feedback-seeking prompt that directs AI to critique an answer rather than replace it.
  3. Evaluate AI feedback by distinguishing specific, checkable corrections from vague praise, and verify at least one claim against a course source.
  4. Compare immediate and delayed retrieval of the same material and explain the difference using memory research.

Student Assignment Sheet

Purpose

There are two ways to use AI when studying. One replaces your memory; the other strengthens it. If you ask AI a question and read its answer, you have practiced reading. If you answer first from memory, let AI critique your attempt, check its critique, and then answer again days later with everything closed — you have practiced remembering. Exams, and most of professional life, test remembering.

This assignment walks you through the second way once, completely, so you can use it for the rest of the semester.

Task

Part A — in one sitting (30 to 40 minutes)

  1. Ask. Receive your question from your instructor or choose one from the provided list. Write it at the top of your worksheet. Before answering, predict your performance: “I expect my answer will be ___ % complete and correct.”
  2. Answer. Close your notes, textbook, and all AI tools. Answer the question from memory in 5 to 8 sentences. Give yourself up to 10 minutes. Mark any part you are unsure about with [?]. An incomplete or partly wrong answer is expected — it is raw material, not a grade.
  3. AI feedback. Open an AI tool and run exactly this prompt, filling in your question and your unedited answer:
    Here is an exam question and my answer, written from memory. Do not rewrite my answer and do not give me a model answer. Instead: (1) tell me what is correct, (2) tell me what is wrong or imprecise, (3) tell me what important content is missing, and (4) ask me one question that would push my understanding further. Question: [question] My answer: [answer]
    Copy the full feedback into your worksheet. If the feedback contains no specific correction or addition — only praise — re-prompt once: “Be more critical. Identify at least two specific weaknesses or omissions.” One re-prompt maximum.
  4. Audit. AI's corrections are claims, not facts. Choose the one piece of feedback you are least sure is right — a correction, an “important missing point,” or, if all you got was praise, the praise itself — and check it against your textbook or lecture notes. Record three lines: the AI claim, the source you checked, and your verdict (confirmed / wrong / more complicated than AI said).
  5. Revise. Close the AI tool. Using only the feedback you decided to accept plus your verification, write your corrected answer. This is your answer key for Part B.

Part B — at least 48 hours later, as scheduled by your instructor

  1. Retrieve. With notes, textbook, AI, and your Part A work all closed, answer the original question from memory one more time.
  2. Compare and reflect. Open your Part A materials. Score your Step 2 answer and your Step 6 answer against your corrected answer from Step 5. Then write a short reflection (150 to 250 words) answering all three:
    • What survived the delay, and what disappeared? How does your Step 1 prediction compare to what actually happened?
    • Look at what you got right in Step 6 that you had wrong or missing in Step 2. Did those pieces come from feedback you merely read, or from corrections you actively worked through in the audit and revision?
    • What is one specific way you will change how you use AI when studying? Name the step of this cycle you are most likely to skip, and what skipping it would cost you.

Guiding Questions

  • Which parts of the AI feedback were specific and checkable, and which were vague encouragement? What is the difference worth to you?
  • Did your [?] marks predict where the feedback found problems — or did the feedback find problems where you felt confident?
  • Why does answering from memory first — even badly — beat reading a great explanation? What does your Part B performance suggest?
  • If AI had simply given you a model answer in Step 3, what would you have lost?

AI Use Rules

AI use allowed: Step 3 only — requesting critique of your own memory-based answer, using the provided prompt, plus at most one critical re-prompt.

Not allowed: Using AI to generate the initial answer (Step 2), the revision (Step 5), the delayed answer (Step 6), or the reflection (Step 7). Using AI to check its own feedback (Step 4 requires a course source). Asking AI for a model answer at any point.

Required disclosure: I answered from memory before using AI. I used AI once (or twice, with one critical re-prompt) to critique my answer. I verified the following AI claim against a course source: _____. All answers and the reflection are my own work.

Privacy rule: Do not enter personal, student, client, patient, participant, workplace, family, or otherwise sensitive information into public AI tools.

Verification rule: At least one piece of AI feedback must be checked against the textbook or lecture notes before it may be used in your revision. AI corrections are claims until checked.

Learning protection rule: The memory-based steps are the assignment. Skipping Step 2 or Step 6 and reconstructing them afterward defeats the purpose and will be visible in your comparison.

Resource rule: Use the simplest prompt that accomplishes the task. Avoid unnecessary back-and-forth, vague prompts that require multiple correction rounds, and full regenerations when targeted edits will do. Efficient AI use is a form of environmental responsibility.

Criteria for Success

Strong work will:

  • show an honest memory-first attempt, including [?] marks and imperfections — a suspiciously polished Step 2 is a weaker submission than a flawed one
  • use the feedback prompt as written, and evaluate the feedback rather than accepting it wholesale
  • verify a genuinely uncertain claim, with a clear verdict grounded in a course source
  • complete the delayed retrieval without reopening materials, and score both attempts honestly
  • draw a specific, personal conclusion in the reflection rather than a generic “AI can be wrong”

Student Submission Checklist

  • question, with your Step 1 prediction
  • Step 2 answer from memory, with [?] marks visible and unedited
  • AI prompt and complete feedback (including the re-prompt, if used)
  • audit record: AI claim, source checked, verdict
  • corrected answer (Step 5)
  • Part B delayed answer (Step 6), unedited
  • comparison scores and reflection answering all three questions
  • AI disclosure statement

Instructor Notes

Why this works for faculty who are skeptical of AI: this is the rare AI assignment in which AI never supplies content. Students generate everything; AI only reacts, and even its reactions must be verified against your course materials. If a student follows the structure, offloading the thinking to AI is not possible. That makes it a good first AI assignment for instructors who worry — reasonably — that AI use replaces learning.

The 48-hour gap must be structural, not honor-system. Collect Part A at the end of a class session (or via an LMS submission that closes), then run Part B at the start of a later session or open the Part B submission window separately. If students can do both parts in one sitting, many will, and the assignment's centerpiece — the visible difference between immediate and delayed retrieval — disappears.

Expect AI to over-praise, and use it. Default AI feedback on a mediocre answer often opens with warm affirmation and hedged, minor suggestions. This is a feature of how these systems are tuned, and it is why Step 3's prompt demands specific corrections and why the audit step exists. In the debrief, ask: “Whose feedback contained no real correction until you demanded it?” Naming this pattern — flattery is not evidence of quality — is one of the most transferable lessons in the assignment. Note that the opposite failure also occurs: AI sometimes introduces its own errors in the corrections. The audit step is there to catch both directions.

Choosing good questions: the assignment works best with questions where student misconceptions are common and checkable against your materials — negative reinforcement vs. punishment, correlation vs. causation, statistical significance vs. practical importance, heritability, how neurons communicate, classical vs. operant conditioning. Avoid pure definition questions (too easy to answer by pattern) and questions your course sources don't clearly settle (the audit step needs an authoritative anchor).

What the research supports, stated carefully: practice testing and the benefits of generating answers are among the best-supported findings in learning research (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Slamecka & Graf, 1978; Dunlosky et al., 2013), and the initial difficulty students feel in Step 2 is the point, not a malfunction (Bjork, 1994). Be more cautious in the debrief about this specific workflow: the full answer-AI feedback-audit-delayed retrieval cycle has not itself been the subject of published trials as of this writing. The honest claim is that the cycle is built from well-established components, and students' own Part A/Part B comparison is their evidence for whether it worked for them.

Relationship to nearby assignments: in The Helpful Wrong Tutor, students start from a polished written explanation and the target skill is appropriate reliance — sorting feedback into accept/reject/verify. Here the target skill is retrieval: the answer starts and ends in memory, and AI never explains anything. The two reinforce each other and can be run in either order. The five-step cycle also works as a lightweight protocol attached to any content assignment — see docs/protocol-pack.md.

Debrief prompt that lands: “Raise your hand if your Step 6 answer was better than your Step 2 answer. Now — who did the work: you, or the AI?” The answer is genuinely both, in a specific division of labor, and getting students to articulate that division is the whole game.

Optional Variations

  • In-class version: Steps 1–2 in the last 15 minutes of one class; Steps 3–5 as homework that night; Steps 6–7 at the start of the next class. The retrieval gap is enforced by the calendar.
  • No-device / provided-feedback version: The instructor collects Step 2 answers, generates AI feedback on three representative (anonymized) answers, and distributes it. Students audit and revise from the provided feedback. This is a legitimate pedagogical choice — including for instructors who limit AI use for ecological reasons — not merely an accessibility fallback.
  • Semester running log: Students repeat the cycle before each exam with one new question, keeping a log of predictions vs. outcomes. Calibration improvement across the semester becomes its own discussion.
  • Pair debrief: Partners exchange Part A packets before Part B and predict what the other student will retain. Predictions about someone else's memory are often better calibrated than predictions about one's own — worth surfacing.
  • Protocol wrapper: Attach Steps 2–4 (answer from memory → feedback, not answers → verify one claim) as a required preface to any existing assignment where students would otherwise ask AI first.
