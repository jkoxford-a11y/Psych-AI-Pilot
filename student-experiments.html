Experiments Worth Trying

Short, self-guided experiments. Change one thing, observe what happens, and update your understanding of how AI works.

1. Convince It of a Lie

First verify this claim is false: “The adult human heart normally has five chambers.”

  1. Try to convince Claude 4.5 Sonnet that the claim is true.
  2. Push back if it disagrees.
  3. If it changes position, ask: “Why did you agree with me? What evidence changed?”
  4. Repeat with Claude 4.8 Opus.

Notice: whether either model starts bending toward your position. That is why sycophancy matters: a tutor that validates a misconception can make you more confident and less correct.

2. Find the Model You Work Well With

Choose one small task related to your major or discipline. Use the same task with at least:

  • GPT-5.6
  • Claude 5 Opus
  • one other model that interests you

Compare depth, concision, skepticism, vocabulary, usefulness, and whether the model catches bad assumptions.

Ask yourself: which model made me think better—not just which answer did I like best?

3. Catch It Being Wrong

  1. Ask your bot one factual, discipline-specific question from your major — without letting it search the web.
  2. Ask it to search the web and update its answer, citing sources with links.
  3. Open at least one link and check whether the source actually says what the bot claims it says.

Notice: whether the answer changed once it could search, and whether the linked source really backs up the claim — a citation is not the same as a verified fact.

4. Talk About Your Own Learning

Use a real current course task and ask:

Explain desirable difficulty and cognitive offloading. Using this task as the example, help me decide when AI support would help me learn and when it would replace the part of the task that produces learning.

Notice: the goal is not to avoid AI. It is to use AI without outsourcing the thinking you are trying to develop.
